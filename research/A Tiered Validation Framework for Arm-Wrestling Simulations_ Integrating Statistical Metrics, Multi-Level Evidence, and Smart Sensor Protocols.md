# A Tiered Validation Framework for Arm-Wrestling Simulations: Integrating Statistical Metrics, Multi-Level Evidence, and Smart Sensor Protocols

This report outlines a comprehensive and rigorous validation framework designed for arm-wrestling biomechanics simulations. The framework is constructed upon three core pillars: a primary focus on statistical agreement metrics for quantitative assessment, a phase-specific hierarchy for integrating diverse experimental data sources, and a set of stringent protocols for smartphone-based measurements. The methodology explicitly excludes the use of injury-risk thresholds as validation targets, instead focusing on the accurate representation of kinematic, kinetic, and neuromuscular behavior. Each component of the framework is designed to ensure transparency, scientific rigor, and defensibility by requiring detailed documentation of evidence, clear labeling of validation status, and a principled approach to handling uncertainty and the absence of consensus thresholds.

## Statistical Agreement Metrics for Model Validation

The validation of any computational model hinges on its ability to accurately represent real-world phenomena. In the context of arm-wrestling biomechanics, this requires a sophisticated, multi-faceted approach to quantifying the agreement between simulated outputs and experimental measurements. The user's directive prioritizes statistical agreement metrics above all else, moving beyond qualitative visual inspection to a quantitative assessment of model fidelity. This section deconstructs the specified suite of statistical tools—Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), Normalized RMSE (nRMSE), Bland–Altman analysis, Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC), cross-correlation, and Dynamic Time Warping (DTW)—and synthesizes guidance from established literature to define their application within the proposed framework. The central thesis is that no single metric provides a complete picture; rather, their combined application offers a powerful toolkit for dissecting and understanding model error across amplitude, bias, temporal alignment, and overall pattern correlation. This approach aligns with modern best practices in verification and validation (V&V), which advocate for a comprehensive suite of metrics to build confidence in a model's predictive capability [[5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28285744/), [15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4321112/)].

The foundation of quantitative validation rests on metrics that quantify the magnitude of the difference between simulation and experiment. RMSE is a fundamental deterministic metric used for this purpose [[17](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12034644/), [18](https://minerva-access.unimelb.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/f812951a-6f9c-4bac-86f1-ef0b1a79f530/content)]. It calculates the square root of the average of the squared differences between predicted and observed values, effectively penalizing larger errors more heavily. Its primary advantage is its intuitive interpretation: it represents the standard deviation of the prediction errors. However, its absolute value is scale-dependent, making it difficult to compare errors across different subjects or variables without normalization. To address this, Normalized RMSE (nRMSE) is employed. The nRMSE expresses the RMSE as a percentage of a relevant reference value, such as the range of the measured data or the mean of the measurements [[17](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12034644/)]. This normalization allows for meaningful comparisons of model performance across different datasets or individuals. For instance, an nRMSE of 10% is often considered a good level of agreement in musculoskeletal modeling, though this can vary depending on the specific variable and task [[17](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12034644/)]. One study on knee joint torque estimation reported nRMSE values ranging from 5.9% to 11.2%, providing a potential benchmark for what might be considered acceptable accuracy for certain biomechanical variables [[17](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12034644/)]. When developing the validation framework, these values should be treated not as rigid pass/fail thresholds but as initial indicators that inform further regression or sensitivity analysis when consensus thresholds are absent [[15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4321112/)].

While RMSE and nRMSE quantify the *amount* of error, they do not reveal its nature. A critical limitation of simple correlation coefficients (like Pearson's r) is that they measure linear association, not agreement; two perfectly correlated but offset methods yield an r of 1, yet they may be unsuitable for interchangeability [[6](https://metricgate.com/blogs/bland-altman-vs-icc-agreement/)]. The Bland–Altman method is superior for assessing agreement because it directly quantifies the systematic bias and the limits of agreement (LoA) [[6](https://metricgate.com/blogs/bland-altman-vs-icc-agreement/)]. The mean difference represents the systematic bias—the average amount by which the simulation consistently over- or under-predicts the measurement. The LoA, calculated as the mean difference ± 1.96 standard deviations of the differences, define the range within which 95% of individual differences between the two methods are expected to fall [[6](https://metricgate.com/blogs/bland-altman-vs-icc-agreement/)]. This metric is expressed in the original units of measurement, which is invaluable for determining if a given level of disagreement is clinically or practically acceptable [[6](https://metricgate.com/blogs/bland-altman-vs-icc-agreement/)]. For example, if a simulation consistently predicts elbow flexion torque 0.5 Nm higher than a dynamometer, the Bland–Altman plot will clearly display this bias, prompting investigation into potential calibration issues. Furthermore, the plot is ideal for identifying proportional bias, where the disagreement changes with the magnitude of the measurement, a pattern that would be missed by other metrics [[6](https://metricgate.com/blogs/bland-altman-vs-icc-agreement/)]. This makes it an indispensable tool for the proposed framework, directly addressing the need to understand the magnitude and character of disagreement.

To assess the consistency and reliability of measurements, particularly when comparing repeated trials or different systems, the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) is the appropriate metric [[6](https://metricgate.com/blogs/bland-altman-vs-icc-agreement/)]. Unlike Pearson correlation, ICC is designed to measure the degree of absolute agreement among measurements, making it suitable for V&V applications [[6](https://metricgate.com/blogs/bland-altman-vs-icc-agreement/)]. It quantifies agreement as a ratio of variances, specifically the proportion of total variance attributable to differences between subjects [[6](https://metricgate.com/blogs/bland-altman-vs-icc-agreement/)]. Standard benchmarks exist to interpret ICC values: below 0.50 is poor, 0.50-0.75 is moderate, 0.75-0.90 is good, and above 0.90 is excellent [[6](https://metricgate.com/blogs/bland-altman-vs-icc-agreement/)]. The choice of ICC model (e.g., ICC(2,1) for random raters or ICC(3,1) for fixed raters) is crucial. Since the framework involves comparing a simulation to a set of experimental measurements, an absolute agreement model is appropriate because it penalizes systematic bias, just like the Bland–Altman mean difference [[6](https://metricgate.com/blogs/bland-altman-vs-icc-agreement/)]. Studies have demonstrated the utility of ICC in validating motion tracking systems. For example, one study found high agreement (ICC > 0.91) between the MediaPipe markerless pose estimation system and an optoelectronic motion capture system for elbow and shoulder flexion-extension in a general subject population [[1](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/16/3/1202)]. Another study reported excellent reliability (ICC > 0.96) for shoulder motion measurements using MediaPipe compared to goniometers [[7](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2025.1635581/full)]. These examples underscore the importance of selecting the correct ICC model and interpreting the results within the context of the specific application and desired level of agreement.

For dynamic events characteristic of arm wrestling, such as the rapid "hit" phase, timing is as important as magnitude. Cross-correlation and Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) are specialized metrics for analyzing time-series data. Cross-correlation identifies the time lag at which the simulated and experimental signals exhibit the highest similarity [[5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28285744/)]. This is particularly useful for validating the timing of muscle activation (EMG) relative to force production, allowing for the inclusion of the electromechanical delay, which is typically around 100 ms [[15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4321112/)]. By finding the optimal lag, one can determine if the simulation correctly captures the sequence of physiological events. DTW offers a more advanced solution for comparing time-series that may differ in speed or duration [[5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28285744/)]. Instead of forcing a one-to-one correspondence between time points, DTW "warp" s the time axis of one signal to optimally match the other, minimizing the cumulative distance between them. This process inherently separates phase (timing) errors from amplitude (magnitude) errors, providing a more nuanced diagnostic tool than RMSE alone [[5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28285744/)]. One comparative study found a positive linear relationship between DTW results and RMSE/nRMSE, suggesting that DTW complements traditional metrics by adding a temporal dimension to the validation process [[5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28285744/)]. The adoption of both cross-correlation and DTW is therefore recommended to fully characterize the dynamic fidelity of the simulation.

The successful implementation of this framework requires a commitment to defining acceptable validation criteria *a priori*. When, as is highly probable for many arm-wrestling-specific variables, no consensus threshold exists, the framework mandates a forward-looking approach: perform regression analysis to establish relationships between error and input parameters, and conduct sensitivity analyses to quantify how uncertainty in inputs propagates to outputs [[15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4321112/)]. This aligns with the principles of formal V&V standards, which emphasize defining acceptance criteria within a Context of Use (COU) and using sensitivity analysis to manage uncertainty when direct validation data is insufficient [[16](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1046202324000665), [28](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/11/5/1983)].

## Hierarchical Framework for Experimental Data Integration

A cornerstone of the proposed validation framework is the establishment of a strict, phase-specific hierarchy for integrating experimental data. Given the profound scarcity of direct biomechanical data specific to competitive arm wrestling, this hierarchical structure is not merely a preference but a necessity for building a credible model. The hierarchy prioritizes evidence based on its relevance and fidelity, creating a logical pathway from gold-standard, sport-specific measurements down to more generalized, albeit indirect, data sources. This approach ensures that the model is first held to the highest possible standard of evidence before falling back on less precise but still valuable information from related fields. The principle of isolating and testing subsystems, a recommendation from ASME guidelines, underpins this structure, allowing for the identification of specific sources of error [[29](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3361760/)]. The hierarchy is tiered as follows: 1) Arm-wrestling-specific experiments (highest priority), 2) General maximal isometric upper-limb studies (for holding/pinning phases), 3) General dynamic upper-limb studies (for transitions/velocity), and 4) Clinical/rehabilitation or general strength data (as indirect kinematic constraints).

The top tier of the hierarchy consists of experiments conducted specifically on the arm-wrestling task. This category represents the ideal validation data, as it captures the unique combination of joint configurations, loading patterns, synergistic muscle activation, and skilled motor control inherent to the sport. While the provided sources indicate that some research has been done, the available data appears limited. Studies have utilized surface electromyography (sEMG) to identify key muscles involved, including the pectoralis major, biceps brachii, pronator teres, and flexor carpi ulnaris [[20](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/16/2/713), [23](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/cpa/article/view/4824)]. Research has also established the high inter-test reliability of arm-wrestling-specific strength tests, with very high intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) for maximal voluntary force (Fmax) and moderate-to-high ICCs for explosive force development (Rate of Force Development, RFDmax) [[21](https://casopisi.junis.ni.ac.rs/index.php/FUPhysEdSport/article/view/7871), [22](https://www.academia.edu/127417274/Reliability_of_Specific_Tests_of_Strength_of_Sports_Arm_Wrestling)]. One study introduced "Mean Jerk" (MJ) as a novel metric for movement smoothness, with a baseline value of 3.37 ± 0.797 m/s³ in arm wrestlers, offering a potential target for optimizing motor control in the simulation [[20](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/16/2/713)]. However, there is a significant gap in the availability of high-fidelity, instrumented data, such as net joint moments, ground reaction forces, and detailed 3D kinematics recorded during actual matches. The greatest contribution of this validation framework will be to formally define the requirements for this missing data, guiding future primary research efforts. Without this top-tier data, the validation process is incomplete and relies on potentially misleading assumptions.

When sport-specific data is unavailable, the second tier of the hierarchy calls for general maximal isometric upper-limb studies to validate the static or quasi-static holding and pinning phases of arm wrestling. This type of data is highly relevant because the pinning phase is characterized by sustained muscular contractions against a resisting force. Several sources provide valuable data for this purpose. Studies have meticulously measured the maximal voluntary isometric torque-angle relationship for elbow flexors and extensors, performed in the transverse plane at a 90° humerus elevation angle—a configuration that closely mimics the arm position during arm wrestling [[8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1050641110000660), [11](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/isometric-torque-angle-relationships-of-the-elbow-flexors-and-ext/)]. These studies found that peak elbow extension torque occurs at approximately 55° of elbow flexion, a specific, testable hypothesis for the simulation [[11](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/isometric-torque-angle-relationships-of-the-elbow-flexors-and-ext/)]. Furthermore, these studies provide normative data for maximum isometric torque at the elbow, which is around 7 kg-m for men and 3.5 kg-m for women for flexion, and about 4 kg-m for men and 2 kg-m for women for extension [[10](https://musculoskeletalkey.com/functional-evaluation-of-the-elbow/)]. Grip force data is also available, with norms of ~7 kg-m for men and ~3.5 kg-m for women, which can serve as a benchmark for the simulation's final gripping action [[10](https://musculoskeletalkey.com/functional-evaluation-of-the-elbow/)]. Finally, these isometric contraction studies often include surface electromyography (sEMG) data, measuring agonist and antagonist muscle activation levels at various joint angles, which can be used to validate the simulated muscle recruitment patterns during the holding phase [[8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1050641110000660)]. It is critical, however, to acknowledge the mismatch between the experimental setup and the real-world task. For instance, the transverse plane elbow torque measurements were taken with the arm at 90° shoulder elevation, which simplifies gravitational effects but may not perfectly replicate the complex 3D loading of arm wrestling [[8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1050641110000660)]. Every piece of data from this tier must be explicitly qualified with these limitations.

The third tier addresses the dynamic, high-velocity transition ("hit") phase of arm wrestling. Here, general dynamic upper-limb studies become the primary source of validation data. While no study perfectly replicates the arm wrestling gesture, research on ballistic upper-limb movements provides relevant physiological principles. A study on wheelchair basketball players characterized the upper limb force-velocity profile using three distinct protocols: a sprint test, a horizontal ballistic push-off test, and a crank ergometer test [[13](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/13/13/7469)]. Although the movement patterns differ, the underlying physics of power generation are comparable. The study found strong correlations between maximal theoretical power (P0) and maximal force (F0) across the different tests, suggesting that these performance characteristics are relatively consistent for an individual athlete [[13](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/13/13/7469)]. This implies that a simulation's ability to generate rapid force could be validated against such a profile. Furthermore, the concept of movement smoothness, operationalized as Mean Jerk (MJ), offers a unique and powerful validation target [[20](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/16/2/713)]. A lower MJ signifies a smoother, more optimized trajectory, which is indicative of superior joint stability and refined motor unit recruitment [[20](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/16/2/713)]. Validating a simulation against this metric would demonstrate not just accurate force production, but also a plausible and efficient motor control strategy. The main challenge with this tier of data is the significant mismatch in task, joint configuration, and velocity. The validity of transferring these findings depends on a careful justification of the similarities in the underlying neuromuscular physiology versus the differences in the mechanical task.

The final, fourth tier of the hierarchy consists of clinical and general strength data, which serves primarily to impose indirect kinematic constraints on the model. This data is too general to validate dynamic behaviors but is essential for ensuring the model remains physically plausible. For example, standard clinical goniometry provides widely accepted norms for functional joint ranges of motion (ROM). The accepted standard for functional elbow motion is a 100-degree arc, and for forearm rotation, it is 100 degrees total, equally divided between pronation and supination [[10](https://musculoskeletalkey.com/functional-evaluation-of-the-elbow/)]. These norms can be programmed as hard limits within the simulation to prevent the generation of non-physiological postures. Similarly, reliable measures of the passive torque-angle relationship for joints like the shoulder can inform the simulation's ligamentous boundary constraints, preventing hyperextension or excessive rotation [[9](https://www.academia.edu/112933483/Developing_reliable_measures_of_the_passive_torque_angle_relationship_for_shoulder_internal_and_external_rotation_Implications_for_overhead_athletics)]. Other sources provide normative data for isometric shoulder internal rotation strength, which can constrain the model's shoulder torque capabilities [[12](https://www.matassessment.com/blog/shoulder-internal-rotation-ir-90)]. The primary limitation of this tier is the substantial mismatch in almost every parameter. An elbow flexion-extension curve measured in the sagittal plane cannot be directly applied to the horizontal plane of arm wrestling without acknowledging the profound differences in muscle length-tension relationships, moment arms, and gravitational loading [[11](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/isometric-torque-angle-relationships-of-the-elbow-flexors-and-ext/)]. Therefore, all data from this tier must be clearly identified as "indirect evidence" and used solely for constraining the model's state space, never for validating its dynamic response. This hierarchical approach, while challenging due to data scarcity, provides a logically sound and transparent methodology for building confidence in the arm-wrestling biomechanics simulation, one step at a time.

## Smartphone-Based Measurement Protocols and Limitations

The integration of smartphone-based measurement protocols into the validation framework presents both a significant opportunity for accessible, field-deployable data collection and a considerable challenge due to the current technical limitations of markerless pose estimation. The user's goal specifies a dual focus: validating markerless pose estimation algorithms and establishing a protocol for synchronized triggering with external sensors. This section critically evaluates the state-of-the-art for these technologies, drawing on extensive research to delineate their capabilities and, more importantly, their limitations in the context of high-fidelity biomechanical validation. The analysis reveals that while markerless pose estimation from a single camera is currently insufficient for directly validating continuous kinematic variables like joint angles, it holds promise for ancillary tasks such as event detection and sensor synchronization. A successful integration into the framework requires a clear-eyed understanding of these roles and a rigorous validation of the smartphone technology itself before it can be trusted as a source of evidence.

The performance of monocular markerless human pose estimation (HPE) systems is a critical factor. Numerous studies highlight significant accuracy challenges, particularly for the upper limbs. One comprehensive evaluation of 11 open-source HPE models found that none achieved an elbow flexion angle error below 5°, a value often cited as the necessary threshold for correct clinical interpretation [[2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-22626-7)]. The mean absolute error (MAE) for projected elbow flexion angles ranged from 21.5° to 28.9° in 2D and from 16.3° to 26.0° in 3D, figures that are far too large for the detailed validation of a musculoskeletal model [[2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-22626-7)]. Even state-of-the-art systems like MediaPipe, which show more promise, yielded RMSE values of 12.76° to 14.17° for elbow flexion in a general population study, and up to 20.49° for a Paralympic athlete, indicating substantial variability [[1](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/16/3/1202)]. For wrist flexion, the error was even higher, with RMSE values of 8.74° to 9.59° [[1](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/16/3/1202)]. These error magnitudes are problematic because they approach or exceed the full physiological range of motion for some joints. For example, OpenCap, another markerless system, exhibited median RMSEs for upper limb kinematics as high as 13.81-30.02°, significantly higher than for the lower limbs [[24](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/digital-health/articles/10.3389/fdgth.2026.1882536/full)]. The fundamental reasons for this poor performance are well-documented. Monocular vision suffers from inherent depth ambiguity, leading to significant errors in reconstructing 3D body poses, especially for parts that are occluded or far from the camera [[2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-22626-7)]. Self-occlusion, where body parts block the view of others, is a major failure mode for most HPE algorithms [[3](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1050641125000938), [24](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/digital-health/articles/10.3389/fdgth.2026.1882536/full)]. In arm wrestling, where extreme proximal-distal overlap and contact are the norm, occlusion is not an anomaly but a central feature of the task, severely compromising tracking reliability [[24](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/digital-health/articles/10.3389/fdgth.2026.1882536/full)].

Despite these significant shortcomings for continuous kinematic measurement, some studies suggest that derived kinematic variables, such as segment velocity, may be more reliably estimated from video. One study comparing a markerless system to a gold-standard optical motion capture system found that while joint angle agreement was poor, segment velocities showed strong agreement, with RMSD values ≤ 0.17 m/s and coefficient of determination (R²) values ranging from 0.96 to 0.98 [[26](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2022.939980/full)]. This counterintuitive finding suggests that while the absolute position of anatomical landmarks may be inaccurate, the rate of change of their positions (i.e., velocity) can be captured with reasonable fidelity. This opens a potential, albeit limited, role for smartphone video analysis: not as a source for joint angles, but as a way to derive and validate angular velocities or translational velocities of body segments. However, even this capability is not without caveats. Measurement errors for markerless systems have been shown to increase with movement velocity, meaning that the very high-speed "hit" phase of arm wrestling might be subject to greater error [[24](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/digital-health/articles/10.3389/fdgth.2026.1882536/full)]. Therefore, if used for velocity, the output must be treated with caution and accompanied by a detailed report of its known uncertainty and failure modes.

Given the unreliability of markerless pose estimation for generating primary validation data, its role in the framework must be redefined. Rather than being a source for kinematics, its utility lies in two other areas: event detection and sensor synchronization. For event detection, the high temporal resolution of modern smartphone cameras (often 60 Hz or higher) is sufficient to precisely mark the start and end of discrete events, such as the moment an arm wrestler's hand first contacts the mat or the instant of maximal force production. Manual annotation of these events in the video can then serve as a precise temporal reference point to align the entire dataset [[7](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2025.1635581/full)]. This is far more accurate than relying on sensor thresholds alone, which can be noisy and imprecise. For sensor synchronization, smartphones can act as a master timebase. By recording a sharp, identifiable event simultaneously with external sensors (force plates, IMUs, EMG systems), a precise time offset can be calculated. Common synchronization methods include clapping hands at the start of a recording, flashing a light visible to both the phone camera and photodiodes, or using a hardware TTL pulse sent to all devices [[2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-22626-7), [25](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-024-04077-3)]. Once the offset and any jitter or drift are characterized, the smartphone's timestamp can be used to synchronize all other data streams, ensuring they share a common temporal reference. This is a robust and achievable goal for smartphone integration.

Before any smartphone-based protocol can be adopted, the chosen HPE algorithm (e.g., MediaPipe, OpenCV-based solutions) must undergo its own independent validation against a ground-truth system, such as a marker-based optical motion capture (OMC) system. This validation should be conducted under conditions that mimic the target application as closely as possible. A study comparing MediaPipe against an OMC system did precisely this, reporting RMSE and ICC values for various joints and movements [[1](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/16/3/1202)]. Another study validated a commercial markerless system against OMC on elite boxers performing shadow boxing, providing concrete error estimates for upper-limb kinematics in a sport-like setting [[26](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2022.939980/full)]. The results of this preliminary validation become part of the evidence base for the main framework. For example, the report must detail the camera placement, calibration method, occlusion handling strategies (if any), and the measured uncertainty (RMSE, ICC) for each joint. If an algorithm fails to track a landmark, the loss rate should be reported [[3](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1050641125000938)]. This creates a transparent record of the measurement system's performance, allowing for an informed judgment of its suitability for the intended task. [[26](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2022.939980/full)] | • **Error Dependency:** Velocity errors may increase with movement speed, which is a concern for the high-velocity "hit" phase. In conclusion, the role of the smartphone in this validation framework must be carefully circumscribed. It should not be used to generate the primary, high-fidelity data needed to validate the core dynamics of the simulation. Instead, its validated functions should be relegated to the auxiliary tasks of precise event detection and robust sensor synchronization. This pragmatic approach leverages the accessibility of smartphone technology while respecting its significant limitations, ensuring that the integrity of the core validation process is not compromised.

## Structured Validation Testing and Evidence Reporting

A robust validation framework requires not only a defined set of metrics and data sources but also a standardized, transparent process for conducting tests and reporting their outcomes. The user's request for a structured output format is a critical component of this requirement, ensuring that every validation test is traceable, reproducible, and clearly interpretable. This section details the prescribed format for each validation test, linking real-world measurements to simulation variables through a series of intermediate steps involving error metrics and acceptance criteria. It emphasizes the mandatory inclusion of comprehensive metadata for every piece of evidence, including source details, population characteristics, and measurement uncertainties. Furthermore, it defines a clear labeling system—`validated`, `partially validated`, or `unvalidated`—to communicate the degree of confidence in each simulated quantity, thereby providing a nuanced and honest assessment of the model's credibility. This approach is aligned with modern V&V best practices that prioritize transparency and the explicit management of uncertainty [[15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4321112/), [28](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/11/5/1983)].

The core of the validation workflow is a structured output for each test case, following the user-specified format:
REAL-WORLD MEASUREMENT
→ SIMULATION VARIABLE
→ ERROR METRIC
→ ACCEPTABLE VALIDATION CRITERION

Let's break down this structure with an example targeting the holding phase of arm wrestling. The real-world measurement could be the maximal voluntary isometric elbow extension torque measured experimentally from a healthy male subject at a 60° elbow flexion angle [[11](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/isometric-torque-angle-relationships-of-the-elbow-flexors-and-ext/)]. The corresponding simulation variable would be the net elbow extension moment predicted by the musculoskeletal model when commanded to exert maximal isometric effort at the same joint angle. The error metric would be the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) between the simulated moment and the experimental torque value. The acceptable validation criterion must be defined beforehand. For instance, it could be set as an nRMSE of less than 15%. If the simulation's nRMSE falls below this threshold, the test passes. If consensus thresholds are unavailable, the criterion is not a simple pass/fail but a statement of the observed error, which then becomes the basis for sensitivity analysis to understand how variations in model parameters might affect this discrepancy [[15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4321112/)]. This structured approach forces a quantitative and explicit comparison, moving beyond qualitative statements of "good" or "bad" agreement.

Crucially, each validation test must be embedded within a rich metadata context. The user's directive to report the source, population, specimen/subject count, joint configuration, measurement method, and uncertainty for every quantity is essential for scientific rigor. This information should be presented in a dedicated evidence table accompanying each test result. The table must specify whether the evidence comes from a direct arm-wrestling study, a general isometric study, or an indirect clinical source. This directly supports the hierarchical data integration strategy. For example, the evidence table for the elbow torque validation test would cite the study by [Author et al., Year], note the population (e.g., 11 healthy subjects, mean age 32 years [[8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1050641110000660)]), describe the measurement method (e.g., isometric torque measurement in the transverse plane using a dynamometer [[11](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/isometric-torque-angle-relationships-of-the-elbow-flexors-and-ext/)]), and state the reported uncertainty (e.g., SD of the torque-angle curve). This level of detail allows reviewers and future users to critically appraise the relevance and quality of the evidence used for validation.

Based on the quality and relevance of the evidence, each simulation variable must be assigned a clear validation status label. The user specified three categories: `validated`, `partially validated`, or `unvalidated`.

*   **Validated:** A quantity is `validated` if it has been successfully compared against high-quality, direct evidence (Tier 1 of the hierarchy) or if it has been compared against high-quality indirect evidence (Tier 2 or 3) and meets a pre-defined acceptance criterion. For example, if the simulation's predicted elbow flexion torque-angle curve falls within two standard deviations of data from a general isometric study under a relevant joint configuration, the elbow flexion moment would be labeled `validated` [[15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4321112/)].

*   **Partially Validated:** A quantity is `partially validated` if the validation is based on evidence with significant limitations or if it meets a criterion that is not universally accepted but is supported by preliminary data. This label indicates that the model has been tested, but confidence is conditional. For instance, if the simulation's predicted forearm pronation torque is compared against data from a general strength study, but the study was conducted in a different plane of motion, the torque would be labeled `partially validated`. The evidence table would explicitly document the mismatch in joint configuration and loading to explain the qualifier.

*   **Unvalidated:** A quantity is `unvalidated` if it has not yet been subjected to any form of quantitative validation against experimental data. This is the default status for any simulation output until proven otherwise. For example, the predicted activation of a small intrinsic hand muscle, for which no experimental data exists, would remain `unvalidated`. This label is critical for transparency, as it honestly communicates the boundaries of the model's verified capabilities.

This structured approach transforms validation from a subjective exercise into a systematic, auditable process. It ensures that every claim of model accuracy is backed by specific, traceable evidence and that the inherent limitations and uncertainties are made explicit. This level of transparency is paramount for building trust in the simulation's predictions and for guiding future research aimed at filling the gaps in our knowledge. By adhering to this rigorous reporting standard, the framework provides a clear path toward achieving a high degree of credibility for the arm-wrestling biomechanics simulation, grounded in the best available evidence and a principled approach to uncertainty.

## Synthesis of a Comprehensive Validation Methodology

The development of a robust validation framework for arm-wrestling biomechanics simulations necessitates a synthesis of disparate components into a cohesive and actionable methodology. This final section integrates the preceding discussions on statistical metrics, data hierarchies, and measurement protocols into a unified strategy. The overarching goal is to create a process that is scientifically rigorous, transparent in its assumptions, and pragmatic in its application, especially given the current landscape of sparse, direct biomechanical data for the sport. The proposed methodology is not a static checklist but a dynamic process of building confidence in the model's predictive capabilities, one validated component at a time. It embraces the principles of formal Verification, Validation, and Uncertainty Quantification (VVUQ), framing the process as a journey toward quantifying model credibility rather than seeking a binary pass/fail verdict [[28](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/11/5/1983), [29](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3361760/)].

The methodology begins with the formulation of a clear Question of Interest (QOI) and a corresponding Context of Use (COU), as advocated by standards like ASME V&V 40 [[16](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1046202324000665)]. The QOI might be to predict the net joint moments at the elbow and shoulder during the pinning phase of an arm-wrestling match. The COU would then define the specific conditions of use, such as the population (elite male competitors), the expected range of motion, and the anticipated force levels. This step is critical because it dictates the type and quality of evidence required for validation. Based on the QOI and COU, the first step is to seek out Tier 1 evidence: direct, arm-wrestling-specific experiments. This involves a diligent search for published studies or, ideally, the initiation of new primary data collection campaigns using high-fidelity instrumentation like marker-based motion capture, force plates, and EMG systems [[19](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12407736/)]. The goal is to acquire gold-standard data on joint kinematics, kinetics, and muscle activity throughout all phases of the arm-wrestling gesture.

Should Tier 1 data prove insufficient, the methodology proceeds down the hierarchical ladder. For the static or quasi-static holding/pinning phase, the next logical step is to gather Tier 2 evidence from general maximal isometric upper-limb studies [[8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1050641110000660), [11](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/isometric-torque-angle-relationships-of-the-elbow-flexors-and-ext/)]. The validation process now shifts to comparing the simulation's predicted isometric torque-angle curves, grip forces, and EMG patterns against this broader, but still relevant, dataset. The critical task here is to meticulously document the mismatch between the experimental conditions (e.g., plane of motion, gravitational loading) and the simulation's intended application, leading to a `partially validated` status if discrepancies are significant [[11](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/isometric-torque-angle-relationships-of-the-elbow-flexors-and-ext/)]. For the dynamic transition ("hit") phase, the methodology turns to Tier 3 data from general dynamic upper-limb studies, such as force-velocity profiles from ballistic tasks [[13](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/13/13/7469)]. The validation focuses on whether the simulation can replicate the physiological principles of rapid force generation, perhaps using metrics like Mean Jerk to assess motor control strategy [[20](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/16/2/713)]. Again, the significant task is to justify the transferability of these findings to the unique arm-wrestling movement pattern. Finally, Tier 4 clinical and general strength data serves as a crucial safety net, imposing indirect kinematic constraints on the model to ensure it does not produce physically impossible motions, such as exceeding normal joint ranges of motion [[10](https://musculoskeletalkey.com/functional-evaluation-of-the-elbow/)].

Throughout this process, the specified suite of statistical metrics is applied rigorously. For each test, the simulation's output is quantitatively compared against the experimental data using RMSE/nRMSE for amplitude, Bland-Altman for bias, ICC for reliability, and DTW/cross-correlation for temporal alignment [[5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28285744/), [6](https://metricgate.com/blogs/bland-altman-vs-icc-agreement/)]. When no consensus threshold for an acceptable error exists—a likely scenario for many arm-wrestling-specific variables—the methodology prescribes a move beyond simple validation. It requires the use of regression analysis to model the relationship between error and input parameters, and sensitivity analysis to propagate uncertainty through the model [[15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4321112/)]. This transforms a failed validation test into a valuable insight about the model's uncertainty. The ASME V&V standards support this, advocating for sensitivity analysis to quantify uncertainty when consensus data is lacking [[15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4321112/)]. The ultimate goal of this process is to construct a statistical model of the simulation's error, allowing for the prediction of a range of possible outcomes (a probability box or p-box) for new, untested scenarios, thus providing a much richer assessment of credibility than a single deterministic prediction [[28](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/11/5/1983)].

Finally, the role of smartphone-based measurements is integrated pragmatically. Before any smartphone data is used, the chosen pose estimation algorithm must be independently validated against a ground-truth system under relevant conditions [[1](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/16/3/1202), [26](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2022.939980/full)]. Its validated role is not as a primary source for kinematics but as a tool for precise event detection (marking the start of the "hit" phase) and robust sensor synchronization. The protocol for this must be clearly documented, including the synchronization method (e.g., hand clap, TTL pulse), the measured time offset, and any jitter or drift [[2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-22626-7), [25](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-024-04077-3)]. The output of this validation—its RMSE, ICC, and known failure modes—becomes part of the evidence base for the main framework.

In conclusion, this synthesized methodology provides a comprehensive and defensible roadmap for validating an arm-wrestling biomechanics simulation. It systematically builds confidence by leveraging a hierarchy of evidence, from the most specific to the most general. It employs a powerful suite of statistical tools to quantitatively assess model performance and explicitly manages uncertainty through sensitivity analysis when definitive answers are not yet available. By mandating rigorous, transparent reporting of all evidence and validation statuses, it ensures that the model's capabilities and limitations are clearly understood. While the current lack of direct, high-fidelity data for arm wrestling represents a significant hurdle, this framework provides the necessary structure to guide future research, identify critical data gaps, and progressively refine the simulation until it achieves a high degree of credibility for its intended use.
