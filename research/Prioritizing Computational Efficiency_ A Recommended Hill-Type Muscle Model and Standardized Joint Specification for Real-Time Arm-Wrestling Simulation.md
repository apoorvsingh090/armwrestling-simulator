# Prioritizing Computational Efficiency: A Recommended Hill-Type Muscle Model and Standardized Joint Specification for Real-Time Arm-Wrestling Simulation

## Comparative Analysis of Muscle Models for Real-Time Simulation

The selection of an appropriate muscle model is a foundational decision in the development of any musculoskeletal simulator, particularly one intended for real-time interaction at frequencies exceeding 500 Hz. The core challenge lies in navigating the inherent trade-off between physiological fidelity and computational tractability. The available literature presents a clear hierarchy of models, ranging from purely phenomenological descriptions to complex biophysical simulations, each with distinct advantages and disadvantages for this specific application [[1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S002192901830856X), [24](https://animatlab.com/Help/Documentation/Biomechanical-Editor/Biomechanical-Body-Parts/Linear-Hill-Muscle)]. The primary contenders for this research goal are Hill-type models, which have become the de facto standard in biomechanics due to their balance of simplicity and predictive power, and Huxley-type models, which offer a deeper physiological basis at a significant computational cost [[2](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rsif/article/20/199/20220430/90380/Numerical-instability-of-Hill-type-muscle), [4](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/hill-type-muscle-model)]. Phenomenological models represent a broad category that often encompasses simplified Hill-type frameworks [[10](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2012.00358/pdf)].

Hill-type muscle models are fundamentally phenomenological, meaning they describe the macroscopic input-output behavior of muscle without attempting to explicitly model the underlying biochemical processes [[24](https://animatlab.com/Help/Documentation/Biomechanical-Editor/Biomechanical-Body-Parts/Linear-Hill-Muscle)]. Proposed by A.V. Hill in 1938, these models treat the muscle-tendon unit as a "black box," focusing on mapping neural excitation to mechanical force output under various length and velocity conditions [[6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hill%27s_muscle_model), [31](http://www.personal.rdg.ac.uk/~shshawin/teaching/biomechanics/wk9control.html)]. Their structure is typically a rheological arrangement, most commonly a Voigt-Kelvin configuration, which consists of a contractile element (CE) in parallel with a parallel elastic element (PEE), and this combination is in series with a series elastic element (SEE) [[3](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.10.14.512218v2.full-text)]. The CE represents active force generation from actin-myosin cross-bridges, the PEE models passive tension from connective tissues like fascia, and the SEE models the elasticity of the tendon [[6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hill%27s_muscle_model)]. The key behavioral characteristics—activation dynamics, force-length relationship, and force-velocity relationship—are defined by empirical or semi-empirical equations rather than first principles [[11](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.10.14.512218v4.full-text)]. This approach has proven immensely valuable; Hill-type models are widely used in forward dynamics simulations of tasks such as jumping, cycling, and locomotion precisely because they are computationally efficient and require a relatively small number of parameters [[1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S002192901830856X), [4](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/hill-type-muscle-model)]. However, their purely phenomenological nature means they lack a direct physiological basis and may not capture all nuances of muscle behavior, such as history-dependent phenomena like short-range stiffness [[1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S002192901830856X), [5](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1014748)]. Despite these known limitations, their conceptual simplicity and low computational burden have solidified their status as the dominant choice in large-scale musculoskeletal modeling for over half a century [[4](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/hill-type-muscle-model)].

In stark contrast, Huxley-type models represent a reductionist, or biophysical, approach grounded in the sliding filament theory of muscle contraction [[24](https://animatlab.com/Help/Documentation/Biomechanical-Editor/Biomechanical-Body-Parts/Linear-Hill-Muscle)]. Developed by Andrew Huxley and Rolf Niedergerke, these models attempt to simulate the actual mechanics of individual actin-myosin cross-bridges, tracking their attachment, force generation, and detachment cycles [[28](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2896253/)]. This provides a much more profound physiological basis for predicting muscle behavior, as it can theoretically link mechanical output to metabolic energy expenditure [[1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S002192901830856X)]. These models are conceptually appealing because they aim to unify mechanical behavior with the underlying biochemistry [[1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S002192901830856X)]. They can accurately capture complex phenomena that simpler Hill models miss, such as the changes in short-range stiffness that occur across different loading conditions [[29](https://read.qxmd.com/read/42678999/cross-bridge-model-for-predicting-muscle-short-range-stiffness-during-movement)]. However, this increased fidelity comes at a steep price: computational complexity. Huxley-type models are mathematically described by partial differential equations (PDEs), not the ordinary differential equations (ODEs) that govern most Hill-type models [[1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S002192901830856X)]. This makes them significantly more difficult to solve and integrate into large, multi-joint dynamic systems. Zahalak (1981) highlighted that even approximations of these models were challenging to apply in large-scale simulations, a limitation that has severely hindered their widespread adoption [[1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S002192901830856X)]. Recent advancements have focused on reformulating these models into state-space forms to improve their tractability, but they remain orders of magnitude more computationally demanding than Hill-type models [[1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S002192901830856X)]. For instance, single time-step simulations of multi-scale finite element models using Huxley mechanics can take thousands of seconds to execute, making them entirely unsuitable for real-time applications [[23](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010482522006965)]. While they predict more realistic patterns of muscle activation and force, their prohibitive computational cost necessitates their relegation to offline analysis and validation roles [[8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22571551/), [23](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010482522006965)].

Phenomenological models, as a broad category, encompass approaches that describe the observable behavior of a system without necessarily implying a specific physical mechanism [[10](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2012.00358/pdf)]. In the context of muscle modeling, many contemporary models are still considered extensions of the Hill framework, incorporating additional features to improve accuracy [[3](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.10.14.512218v2.full-text)]. For example, some models include separate force-velocity curves for concentric and eccentric contractions, viscoelastic damping elements to account for energy dissipation, or activation dynamics that are dependent on muscle length [[11](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.10.14.512218v4.full-text), [30](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12938-017-0399-7)]. While these refinements enhance physiological realism, they do not change the fundamental ODE-based structure of Hill-type models. Therefore, they remain computationally tractable options for real-time simulation, though they may be less efficient than the simplest Hill formulations. The distinction is crucial: while Huxley-type models are fundamentally different in their mathematical description (PDEs vs. ODEs), extended Hill-type models are refinements of the same basic ODE framework. For the purpose of achieving high-frequency real-time control, the computational advantage of the Hill-type ODE structure remains paramount, regardless of how many phenomenological enhancements are added. The literature consistently identifies Hill-type models as the most practical choice for real-time musculoskeletal simulation due to their superior computational efficiency compared to Huxley-type models [[4](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/hill-type-muscle-model), [24](https://animatlab.com/Help/Documentation/Biomechanical-Editor/Biomechanical-Body-Parts/Linear-Hill-Muscle)]. Huxley models demand vast computational resources in both memory and processing time, primarily because the microscale calculations are the most time-consuming part of any multi-scale simulation [[23](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010482522006965)]. Even with modern parallelization techniques on high-performance computing clusters, execution times remain long, precluding their use in interactive systems [[23](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010482522006965)]. Consequently, the strategy of using a simplified Hill-type model for the real-time loop and a more complex Huxley-type model for subsequent offline validation is a well-supported and logical approach, aligning with established practices in the field [[2](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rsif/article/20/199/20220430/90380/Numerical-instability-of-Hill-type-muscle), [8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22571551/)].

## Minimal Computational Model for Real-Time Implementation

To achieve the target of real-time control loops exceeding 500 Hz, the muscle model must be optimized for computational speed above all else, while retaining the essential biomechanical behaviors required to drive the simulator realistically. Based on a synthesis of the provided research, a simplified, excitation-driven Hill-type model emerges as the optimal choice for this task [[24](https://animatlab.com/Help/Documentation/Biomechanical-Editor/Biomechanical-Body-Parts/Linear-Hill-Muscle), [33](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2021.642742/full)]. This model prioritizes efficiency by employing several strategic simplifications that reduce computational load, trading some physiological detail for the speed necessary for high-frequency integration. The core of this minimal model consists of three primary functional relationships—activation dynamics, force-length, and force-velocity—which are implemented with computationally inexpensive functions. Furthermore, critical structural choices, such as the representation of tendon compliance, will be made to maximize performance. Each component is detailed below with its corresponding equation, variable definitions, units, assumptions, and limitations, grounded in the referenced literature.

First, **Activation Dynamics (AD)**, which describes the transition from neural excitation to active muscle force, is a major source of computational cost in traditional Hill-type models. A common implementation involves integrating a first-order ordinary differential equation (ODE) to determine the time-history-dependent activation level, `A(t)` [[3](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.10.14.512218v2.full-text), [11](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.10.14.512218v4.full-text)]. While physiologically plausible, this integration step is computationally expensive within a tight control loop. To eliminate this overhead, the minimal model should adopt an *excitation-driven* formulation. In this approach, the model bypasses the ODE integration and uses the raw neural excitation signal, `E`, directly as the activation multiplier [[33](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2021.642742/full)]. A study demonstrated that an excitation-driven Hill-type model was approximately 9.6 Hz faster than an activation-driven version on the same hardware, highlighting the significant speed advantage [[33](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2021.642742/full)]. The equation for total muscle force is modified to remove the explicit activation term `A(t)` and replace it with the excitation input `E`.

$$ F_{total} = E \cdot f_L(L_{CE}) \cdot f_v(v_{CE}) \cdot F_{max} $$

*   $F_{total}$: Total force generated by the muscle-tendon unit (MTU). **Units:** Newtons (N).
*   $E$: Neural excitation level. **Units:** Dimensionless, typically ranging from 0 (no excitation) to 1 (full excitation) [[33](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2021.642742/full)].
*   $f_L(L_{CE})$: Normalized Force-Length relationship function. **Units:** Dimensionless.
*   $f_v(v_{CE})$: Normalized Force-Velocity relationship function. **Units:** Dimensionless.
*   $F_{max}$: Maximum isometric force of the muscle. **Units:** Newtons (N) [[6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hill%27s_muscle_model)].
*   $L_{CE}$: Length of the Contractile Element (CE). **Units:** Meters (m) or millimeters (mm) [[6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hill%27s_muscle_model)].
*   $v_{CE}$: Contraction velocity of the CE. **Units:** m/s or mm/s [[6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hill%27s_muscle_model)].

**Assumption:** The excitation signal `E` is assumed to be a perfect proxy for the intracellular calcium concentration and instantaneous active stress generation, ignoring the time delays associated with excitation-contraction coupling [[33](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2021.642742/full)]. This assumption reduces physiological fidelity but is a critical simplification for real-time performance.

**Limitation:** This approach fails to capture important phenomena like fatigue, post-activation potentiation, and the characteristic rise and decay time courses of muscle activation and deactivation observed in electromyography (EMG) signals [[3](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.10.14.512218v2.full-text)]. It assumes an immediate and proportional force response to the input signal.

**Citation:** This excitation-driven approach is noted as being computationally more efficient, and a similar method is used in AnimatLab where activation is modeled via a sigmoidal stimulus-tension function derived from a non-spiking neuron [[24](https://animatlab.com/Help/Documentation/Biomechanical-Editor/Biomechanical-Body-Parts/Linear-Hill-Muscle), [33](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2021.642742/full)].

Second, the **Force-Length (FL) Relationship**, which describes how the maximum active force a muscle can generate depends on its current length, is typically modeled using a bell-shaped curve [[24](https://animatlab.com/Help/Documentation/Biomechanical-Editor/Biomechanical-Body-Parts/Linear-Hill-Muscle)]. The most common and computationally efficient forms are Gaussian or quadratic functions [[3](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.10.14.512218v2.full-text)]. A normalized Gaussian function is an excellent choice for the minimal model.

$$ f_L(L_{CE}) = \exp\left(-0.5 \cdot \left(\frac{L_{CE} - L_{opt}}{\sigma}\right)^2\right) $$

*   $f_L(L_{CE})$: Normalized force-length function value. **Units:** Dimensionless.
*   $L_{CE}$: Current length of the contractile element. **Units:** m or mm.
*   $L_{opt}$: Optimal length of the contractile element, where active force is maximal. **Units:** m or mm [[6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hill%27s_muscle_model)].
*   $\sigma$: A parameter determining the width of the bell curve. **Units:** m or mm [[3](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.10.14.512218v2.full-text)].

**Assumption:** The shape of the FL curve is symmetric and perfectly represented by a Gaussian distribution. This is a simplification, as experimentally measured curves can exhibit asymmetry, particularly on the descending limb [[3](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.10.14.512218v2.full-text)].

**Limitation:** This simple form does not account for potential length-dependency of activation (ADL), where the sensitivity of the contractile machinery to calcium can change with muscle length [[11](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.10.14.512218v4.full-text)]. It also ignores the contribution of passive elasticity from the PEE to the total force at lengths greater than $L_{opt}$, which is a feature of more complete models [[3](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.10.14.512218v2.full-text), [6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hill%27s_muscle_model)].

**Citation:** Quadratic and Gaussian functions are cited as common mathematical descriptions for the FL relationship [[3](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.10.14.512218v2.full-text)]. Some studies use a more complex exponential form, $F_{isom}(l_{CE}) = F_{max} / [\exp(-\nu \cdot ((l_{CE} - l_{opt}) / \Delta W))]$, to allow for different shapes on the ascending and descending limbs [[30](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12938-017-0399-7)].

Third, the **Force-Velocity (FV) Relationship**, which defines how shortening or lengthening velocity affects the force a muscle can produce, is famously described by Hill's hyperbolic equation [[6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hill%27s_muscle_model)]. For a real-time model, the standard two-parameter hyperbola is acceptable, but further simplification can yield significant gains in speed. One highly effective simplification is to use a linear approximation of the FV relationship, a technique employed in the AnimatLab Linear Hill Muscle model [[24](https://animatlab.com/Help/Documentation/Biomechanical-Editor/Biomechanical-Body-Parts/Linear-Hill-Muscle)]. This replaces the computationally more intensive division operation in the hyperbola with a simple linear function.

$$ f_v(v_{CE}) = 1 - \frac{v_{CE}}{v_{max}} $$

This linear approximation is valid only for a limited range of velocities around zero (the isometric point).

*   $f_v(v_{CE})$: Normalized force-velocity function value. **Units:** Dimensionless.
*   $v_{CE}$: Contraction velocity of the contractile element. **Units:** m/s or mm/s [[6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hill%27s_muscle_model)].
*   $v_{max}$: Maximum shortening velocity of the muscle fiber. **Units:** m/s or mm/s [[6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hill%27s_muscle_model)].

**Assumption:** The relationship between force and velocity is linear within the operational range of the simulator. This is a gross oversimplification of Hill's hyperbolic law, which correctly predicts that force approaches zero asymptotically as velocity approaches the maximum shortening velocity [[6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hill%27s_muscle_model), [31](http://www.personal.rdg.ac.uk/~shshawin/teaching/biomechanics/wk9control.html)].

**Limitation:** The linear model drastically overestimates force at high shortening velocities and does not capture the true hyperbolic shape of the F-V curve. It also lacks the ability to model eccentric contractions (where the muscle lengthens) effectively, as it would predict negative forces becoming increasingly negative, which is unphysical. More advanced Hill models use double-hyperbolic equations to separately model concentric and eccentric force production [[3](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.10.14.512218v2.full-text)]. The linear approximation sacrifices this accuracy for speed.

**Citation:** The AnimatLab Linear Hill Muscle model explicitly uses a linear approximation for the FV relationship to enhance computational efficiency [[24](https://animatlab.com/Help/Documentation/Biomechanical-Editor/Biomechanical-Body-Parts/Linear-Hill-Muscle)]. The standard hyperbolic form is given by Hill's original work: $F = (F_{max} \cdot v_m) / (v_m + v) - c \cdot v$ [[6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hill%27s_muscle_model)].

Finally, the issue of **Tendon Compliance** must be addressed. The series elastic element (SEE), representing the tendon, stores and releases elastic energy, which is crucial for modeling certain movements like hopping or running [[30](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12938-017-0399-7)]. However, modeling a compliant tendon requires solving additional kinematic constraints to find the length of the CE, which adds computational expense. For the goal of high-speed control (>500 Hz), this cost may be prohibitive. A powerful simplification is to assume a *rigid tendon*, where the MTU length is equal to the CE length, and the force in the SEE is infinite. Studies comparing musculoskeletal models have shown that rigid-tendon models can be 2 to 54 times faster than their elastic-tendon counterparts, depending on the integrator used [[25](https://simtk.org/projects/opensim_muscle)]. While this introduces an error in force prediction—with one study reporting a mean absolute error of ~20% of $F_{max}$—this level of accuracy may be acceptable for a real-time control loop where the primary goal is stable and responsive motion, rather than precise force quantification [[25](https://simtk.org/projects/opensim_muscle)]. The computational savings far outweigh the moderate loss in force accuracy for this specific application.

**Assumption:** The tendon is infinitely stiff, so musculotendon length equals contractile element length ($L_{MTU} = L_{CE}$) and musculotendon velocity equals contractile element velocity ($v_{MTU} = v_{CE}$). This neglects the storage and release of elastic energy in the tendon, which is a significant factor in fast, ballistic movements [[30](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12938-017-0399-7)].

**Limitation:** The model cannot predict phenomena related to tendon compliance, such as force lag during rapid stretches, stretch-shortening cycles, or the dampening of oscillations caused by tendon elasticity. The predicted forces may be less accurate, especially during dynamic contractions [[25](https://simtk.org/projects/opensim_muscle)].

**Citation:** Research by Millard et al. (2013) benchmarked different musculotendon models and found the rigid-tendon model to be significantly faster than equilibrium elastic tendon models [[25](https://simtk.org/projects/opensim_muscle)]. Another study suggests that for short-tendon muscles, a rigid-tendon model with activation dynamics offers a good balance of accuracy and efficiency [[26](https://ruc.udc.es/entities/publication/104caa40-df68-4d04-abd6-fa94c59904d0)].

By combining these four simplifications—an excitation-driven AD, a Gaussian FL relationship, a linear FV relationship, and a rigid tendon approximation—a highly efficient yet biomechanically meaningful muscle model is created, perfectly suited for the demanding real-time requirements of the arm-wrestling simulator.

## Advanced Muscle Model for Offline Validation

While the minimal Hill-type model is engineered for real-time performance, its simplifications necessarily reduce physiological fidelity. To ensure the credibility and accuracy of the simulation, it is imperative to validate its outputs against a more sophisticated, physiologically representative model. For this purpose, an advanced muscle model should be employed in offline, post-simulation analysis. The most suitable candidate for this role is a Huxley-type cross-bridge model, which provides a detailed, biophysically-based description of force generation at the molecular level [[1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S002192901830856X), [28](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2896253/)]. Such a model can serve as a "gold standard" to evaluate the predictions of the real-time Hill model, particularly concerning muscle forces, activation dynamics, and responses to complex stimuli like rapid stretch-shortening cycles. An alternative, more accessible but still enhanced option would be an extended Hill-type model incorporating multiple advanced features. This section details both options, focusing primarily on the Huxley-type model as the ultimate validation tool.

The Huxley-type model is rooted in the sliding filament theory, simulating the cyclical attachment and detachment of myosin cross-bridges to actin filaments [[24](https://animatlab.com/Help/Documentation/Biomechanical-Editor/Biomechanical-Body-Parts/Linear-Hill-Muscle)]. Instead of using phenomenological equations for force-velocity and activation, it employs a system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) to track the probability of cross-bridges existing in different states (e.g., detached, attached-pre-powerstroke, attached-post-powerstroke) [[28](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2896253/)]. A simple two-state model (attached 'high force' state A, detached 'low/no force' state B) is often insufficient, as it cannot explain all phases of a force transient [[28](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2896253/)]. More robust models use three or more states. For instance, a three-state cyclic model (A → B → C → A) can successfully replicate the three distinct phases of a quick-release experiment: phase 1 (immediate elastic rise), phase 2 (rapid decay), and phase 3 (delayed tension rise) [[28](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2896253/)]. The rates of transition between these states are governed by rate constants that are functions of strain and are calibrated using experimental data, such as sinusoidal length perturbation experiments [[28](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2896253/)]. This allows the model to predict not only steady-state force but also the dynamic transients that occur during changes in length and velocity.

A key advantage of the Huxley-type model is its ability to capture phenomena that are beyond the scope of classical Hill models. For example, it can naturally explain short-range stiffness, the phenomenon where muscle stiffness increases dramatically during small, rapid oscillations around a resting length. This effect is linked to the kinetics of the cross-bridge cycle and is not captured by the simple springs and dashpots of a Hill model [[5](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1014748), [29](https://read.qxmd.com/read/42678999/cross-bridge-model-for-predicting-muscle-short-range-stiffness-during-movement)]. The model can also incorporate the effects of key ligands like ATP, ADP, and inorganic phosphate (Pi), which modulate the cross-bridge cycle and thus influence force production. A six-state model, for example, can explicitly track steps such as ATP binding, the force-generating conformational change (power stroke) involving Pi release, and ADP release [[28](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2896253/)]. This level of detail allows for a more nuanced understanding of muscle energetics and fatigue. The total force generated by the muscle is the sum of the forces from all attached cross-bridges, multiplied by the number of cross-bridges per unit area. This provides a direct link between molecular events and macroscopic force output.

Calibrating a Huxley-type model requires specific kinetic rate constants determined experimentally for the target muscle fibers. For example, for rabbit psoas fibers at 20°C, key constants include k2=440 s⁻¹ (ATP binding/conformational change), k4=56 s⁻¹ (force-generating Pi release step), and k−4=129 s⁻¹ (Pi re-binding) [[28](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2896253/)]. These parameters would need to be obtained for human upper extremity muscles relevant to arm wrestling, such as the Pectoralis Major and Flexor Carpi Ulnaris, whose EMG activity is highest during the sport [[27](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/23672647_Electromyographic_evaluation_of_upper_limb_muscles_involved_in_armwrestling_sport_simulation_during_dynamic_and_static_conditions)]. While computationally intensive, recent advances have led to the development of state-space formulations of Huxley models, which make them more tractable for integration into larger systems [[1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S002192901830856X)]. A study by Lemaire et al. showed that a 2-state Huxley model could describe the mechanical behavior of rat soleus muscle during stretch-shortening cycles with accuracy comparable to a corresponding Hill-type model, demonstrating its potential for validation purposes [[1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S002192901830856X)].

As a more computationally manageable alternative for offline validation, an **extended Hill-type model** can be developed. This model retains the familiar Hill structure but incorporates features that address the limitations of the minimal real-time model. This approach offers a middle ground, providing enhanced physiological realism without the extreme computational burden of a full cross-bridge model. Key features to include in this advanced Hill model are:

1.  **Separate Concentric and Eccentric Force-Velocity Curves:** The standard Hill hyperbola is known to overestimate force during eccentric contractions (when the muscle is lengthening). An advanced model would implement a double-hyperbolic equation or another formulation that provides distinct curves for concentric (shortening) and eccentric (lengthening) actions, allowing for higher force production during lengthening [[3](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.10.14.512218v2.full-text)]. This is critical for accurately simulating the losing player's experience of undergoing eccentric contractions to resist the opponent's pull [[18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8091050/)].

2.  **Viscoelastic Series Damping Element (SDE):** While the minimal model uses a rigid tendon, the extended model would include a compliant SEE in series with a damping element. This SDE helps to dissipate energy and reduce the unphysiological high-frequency oscillations that can arise in simulations with purely elastic elements [[30](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12938-017-0399-7)]. The damping coefficient can be made force- or length-dependent to better mimic the viscoelastic properties of biological tendons [[30](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12938-017-0399-7)].

3.  **Length-Dependent Activation Dynamics (ADL):** The sensitivity of the contractile proteins to calcium can vary with muscle fiber length. An advanced Hill model can incorporate this by adding a length-dependent term, $\rho$, to the activation dynamics equation. This modifies the rate of rise and decay of activation, making it more sensitive in stretched muscles [[11](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.10.14.512218v4.full-text), [30](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12938-017-0399-7)].

4.  **Nonlinear Parallel and Series Elastic Elements:** The passive elements are often modeled as simple springs. An improved model would use nonlinear functions, such as piecewise linear-exponential expressions, to define the stress-strain relationships of the PEE and SEE. This better captures the "toe region" of tendons, where they are initially compliant before engaging their linear, stiff region [[3](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.10.14.512218v2.full-text), [31](http://www.personal.rdg.ac.uk/~shshawin/teaching/biomechanics/wk9control.html)].

5.  **Fatigue Mechanism:** While rarely included in real-time models, a fatigue module can be incorporated into an offline model. This would reduce the maximum isometric force, $F_{max}$, over time based on factors like activation level and contraction frequency, providing a more realistic simulation of muscle exhaustion during a prolonged match [[3](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.10.14.512218v2.full-text), [11](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.10.14.512218v4.full-text)].

By implementing either the detailed Huxley-type model or this advanced extended Hill-type model in an offline environment, the forces, activations, and lengths predicted by the real-time simulator can be rigorously evaluated. Discrepancies between the models can highlight areas where the minimal model's simplifications lead to inaccuracies, guiding future refinements. This two-model strategy—using a fast, simplified model for real-time control and a slow, detailed model for validation—is a robust and scientifically sound methodology for developing a credible biomechanical simulator.

## Definitive Skeletal and Joint Specification

A definitive skeletal and joint specification is essential for creating a physically plausible and controllable arm-wrestling simulator. This specification must define the kinematics of the entire upper limb chain, from the shoulder to the hand, using a consistent and standardized coordinate system. The recommendations of the International Society of Biomechanics (ISB) Standardization and Terminology Committee (STC) provide the ideal foundation for this task, ensuring that the resulting model is anatomically correct and interoperable with other biomechanical research [[12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15844264/), [14](https://www.academia.edu/11753608/ISB_recommendation_on_definitions_of_joint_coordinate_systems_of_various_joints_for_the_reporting_of_human_joint_motion_Part_II_shoulder_elbow_wrist_and_hand)]. The specification will detail the Degrees of Freedom (DoFs), axis orientations, and zero positions for the glenohumeral, elbow, radioulnar, and wrist joints. Critically, it will differentiate between the full anatomical Range of Motion (ROM) and the "feasible ROM" applicable to arm wrestling. As per the research directive, feasible ROM limits will not be arbitrarily invented but will be constrained by documented general joint stability principles, such as ligamentous boundaries and osseous blocks, where available. Any DoFs lacking clear constraints will be explicitly flagged for future empirical measurement, adhering strictly to the provided source material.

**Glenohumeral (Shoulder) Joint**

The glenohumeral joint is a ball-and-socket joint responsible for the complex multi-planar movements of the arm. Its specification is complicated by its inherent instability, which relies heavily on muscular support [[16](https://musculoskeletalkey.com/biomechanics-of-the-upper-limb/)].

*   **Degrees of Freedom (DoFs):** Three rotational DoFs are required to fully describe its motion: axial rotation (internal/external rotation), elevation (flexion/extension in the scapular plane), and plane of elevation (scaption, or movement out of the sagittal plane) [[33](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2021.642742/full)].
*   **ISB Coordinate System & Zero Position:** The ISB recommends a bone-based coordinate system for the humerus [[15](https://orthoload.com/shoulder-joint/)]. The origin is located at the center of the articular surface of the humeral head. The axes are defined as follows: the positive X-axis points anteriorly, the positive Y-axis points superiorly, and the positive Z-axis points laterally, forming a right-handed system [[15](https://orthoload.com/shoulder-joint/)]. The zero position for rotations is typically defined as the anatomical neutral position of the arm at the side of the body.
*   **Anatomical ROM:** The full anatomical range of motion is extensive, allowing for approximately 180° of flexion/extension, 90° of abduction/adduction, and 90° of internal/external rotation [[16](https://musculoskeletalkey.com/biomechanics-of-the-upper-limb/)]. However, the functional arc is much smaller.
*   **Arm-Wrestling Feasible ROM:** In the neutral starting position for arm wrestling, the shoulder is typically flexed at 45° [[18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8091050/)]. The primary motion throughout the match is progressive internal rotation of the humerus, driven by muscles like the pectoralis major and latissimus dorsi, to overcome the opponent's external rotation moment [[18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8091050/)]. The glenohumeral joint's stability is precarious, with only 25-30% of the humeral head in contact with the glenoid fossa in any given position [[16](https://musculoskeletalkey.com/biomechanics-of-the-upper-limb/)]. Its stability is maintained dynamically by the rotator cuff muscles and other surrounding musculature [[16](https://musculoskeletalkey.com/biomechanics-of-the-upper-limb/)]. Direct evidence for specific feasible ROM limits *during arm wrestling* is absent in the provided sources. Therefore, the feasible ROM must be bounded by the anatomical ROM but is fundamentally limited by the capacity of the stabilizing muscles to maintain joint integrity under the significant torsional and compressive loads experienced in the sport [[18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8091050/)]. Spiral fractures of the distal humerus are a known injury, indicating that extreme or poorly controlled torques can exceed bony and soft tissue tolerance [[18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8091050/)]. **Conclusion:** The feasible ROM for internal rotation should be constrained by the anatomical ROM. The exact safe limits under simulated load are unknown and **require future empirical measurement** to define the operational envelope of the simulator and prevent physically unrealistic or injurious joint configurations.

**Elbow Joint**

The elbow is a trochoginglymoid joint, primarily allowing for flexion-extension at the ulnohumeral articulation, combined with pronation-supination from the proximal radioulnar joint [[17](https://openorthopaedicsjournal.com/VOLUME/14/PAGE/95/FULLTEXT/)].

*   **Degrees of Freedom (DoFs):** Primarily one DoF for flexion and extension [[17](https://openorthopaedicsjournal.com/VOLUME/14/PAGE/95/FULLTEXT/)]. Pronation-supination is a motion of the forearm, but the elbow joint capsule constrains it to move in concert with the radius and ulna.
*   **ISB Coordinate System & Zero Position:** The ISB recommends a coordinate system based on palpable landmarks on the humerus and ulna. Typically, the origin is placed near the capitulolumbal groove. The Y-axis points along the longitudinal axis of the humerus, from the capitulum towards the medial epicondyle. The Z-axis is defined by a vector from the origin to the radial head. The X-axis is calculated as the cross product of Y and Z. The zero position for flexion/extension is defined as 90° of elbow flexion [[17](https://openorthopaedicsjournal.com/VOLUME/14/PAGE/95/FULLTEXT/)].
*   **Anatomical ROM:** The normal anatomical range of motion is from 0 degrees (full extension) to approximately 145 degrees of flexion [[17](https://openorthopaedicsjournal.com/VOLUME/14/PAGE/95/FULLTEXT/)].
*   **Arm-Wrestling Feasible ROM:** Throughout an arm-wrestling match, the winning athlete maintains a fixed elbow flexion, with the elbow flexors (biceps brachii, brachialis) undergoing isometric contraction to stabilize the joint [[18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8091050/)]. The losing athlete may eventually be forced into some degree of extension. Stability at the elbow is provided by a combination of static and dynamic components, including the ulnohumeral articulation, the anterior bundle of the medial collateral ligament (MCL), and the lateral ulnar collateral ligament (LUCL) [[17](https://openorthopaedicsjournal.com/VOLUME/14/PAGE/95/FULLTEXT/)]. The MCL is the primary valgus stabilizer of the elbow [[16](https://musculoskeletalkey.com/biomechanics-of-the-upper-limb/), [17](https://openorthopaedicsjournal.com/VOLUME/14/PAGE/95/FULLTEXT/)]. Excessive valgus stress, which occurs during arm wrestling, can lead to MCL injury. For activities of daily living (ADL), a functional arc of 30 to 130 degrees of flexion is sufficient [[16](https://musculoskeletalkey.com/biomechanics-of-the-upper-limb/), [17](https://openorthopaedicsjournal.com/VOLUME/14/PAGE/95/FULLTEXT/)]. **Conclusion:** The feasible ROM for the elbow during arm wrestling is practically constrained to the functional ADL arc of **30° to 130° of flexion**. This range represents the safe and stable operating zone of the joint, respecting its ligamentous constraints. Values outside this range are not supported by evidence for a typical match scenario.

**Radioulnar (Forearm) Joint**

The forearm consists of the proximal radioulnar joint (PRUJ) and the distal radioulnar joint (DRUJ), which together allow for pronation and supination of the forearm.

*   **Degrees of Freedom (DoFs):** One primary DoF for pronation and supination [[17](https://openorthopaedicsjournal.com/VOLUME/14/PAGE/95/FULLTEXT/)].
*   **ISB Coordinate System & Zero Position:** The ISB recommends a specialized intermediate coordinate system for the forearm, defined at the posture of neutral forearm rotation (elbow flexed to 90°, thumb pointing toward the shoulder) [[13](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7893843_ISB_recommendation_on_definitions_of_joint_coordinate_systems_of_various_joints_for_the_reporting_of_human_joint_motion_-_Part_II_Shoulder_elbow_wrist_and_hand), [14](https://www.academia.edu/11753608/ISB_recommendation_on_definitions_of_joint_coordinate_systems_of_various_joints_for_the_reporting_of_human_joint_motion_Part_II_shoulder_elbow_wrist_and_hand)]. The origin of this system coincides with the origin of the radius's coordinate system. The axes are aligned with the ulna's coordinate system at this neutral posture. The primary axis of rotation (e.g., e1 aligned with the ulna's Y-axis) describes pronation/supination [[13](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7893843_ISB_recommendation_on_definitions_of_joint_coordinate_systems_of_various_joints_for_the_reporting_of_human_joint_motion_-_Part_II_Shoulder_elbow_wrist_and_hand)]. The zero position for pronation/supination is defined as the neutral forearm posture [[13](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7893843_ISB_recommendation_on_definitions_of_joint_coordinate_systems_of_various_joints_for_the_reporting_of_human_joint_motion_-_Part_II_Shoulder_elbow_wrist_and_hand)].
*   **Anatomical ROM:** The total anatomical range of rotation is approximately 80 degrees of pronation and 85 degrees of supination [[17](https://openorthopaedicsjournal.com/VOLUME/14/PAGE/95/FULLTEXT/)].
*   **Arm-Wrestling Feasible ROM:** The primary action in the winning athlete's arm is the internal rotation of the upper arm, which translates into forearm pronation [[18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8091050/)]. The losing athlete may be forced into extreme supination. The stability of the radioulnar joints is maintained by several structures, including the annular ligament for the PRUJ and the triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC) for the DRUJ [[16](https://musculoskeletalkey.com/biomechanics-of-the-upper-limb/)]. For ADL, a total of 100 degrees of forearm rotation (50 degrees pronation and 50 degrees supination) is considered sufficient [[16](https://musculoskeletalkey.com/biomechanics-of-the-upper-limb/), [17](https://openorthopaedicsjournal.com/VOLUME/14/PAGE/95/FULLTEXT/)]. **Conclusion:** The feasible ROM for the forearm during arm wrestling is practically constrained to the functional ADL arc of **50° of pronation and 50° of supination**. This range is dictated by the functional requirements of daily life and represents the stable operating limit of the forearm's rotational capability. The anatomical extremes are unlikely to be reached safely in a simulated competitive match.

**Wrist (Radiocarpal) Joint**

The wrist joint, or radiocarpal joint, connects the forearm to the hand and allows for a wide range of motion.

*   **Degrees of Freedom (DoFs):** Two primary DoFs: flexion and extension, and radial and ulnar deviation [[16](https://musculoskeletalkey.com/biomechanics-of-the-upper-limb/)].
*   **ISB Coordinate System & Zero Position:** The ISB defines a coordinate system based on the distal radius. The origin is at the center of the carpal articular surface. The Y-axis points along the long axis of the third metacarpal. The Z-axis is perpendicular to the palmar carpal surface. The X-axis is the cross product of Y and Z. The zero position for flexion/extension is defined as the position where the forearm is vertical (palm facing down). The zero position for radial/ulnar deviation is defined when the third metacarpal system is aligned with the wrist system [[13](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7893843_ISB_recommendation_on_definitions_of_joint_coordinate_systems_of_various_joints_for_the_reporting_of_human_joint_motion_-_Part_II_Shoulder_elbow_wrist_and_hand)].
*   **Anatomical ROM:** The full anatomical range includes approximately 80° of flexion, 70° of extension, 20-25° of radial deviation, and 30-35° of ulnar deviation [[16](https://musculoskeletalkey.com/biomechanics-of-the-upper-limb/)].
*   **Arm-Wrestling Feasible ROM:** In the neutral starting position, the wrist is in semi-supination [[18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8091050/)]. During the late stages of a match, the wrist of the losing athlete will undergo flexion as the pronators and flexors give way [[18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8091050/)]. Ligamentous constraints within the wrist capsule limit its range of motion. One study found that 70% of the maximal wrist motion was required for most ADL, suggesting a functional arc [[16](https://musculoskeletalkey.com/biomechanics-of-the-upper-limb/)]. Specifically, this study identified a functional arc of 40° of extension, 40° of pronation, 17° of radial deviation, and 40° of ulnar deviation at the wrist [[16](https://musculoskeletalkey.com/biomechanics-of-the-upper-limb/)]. **Conclusion:** The feasible ROM for the wrist during arm wrestling is a subset of this functional arc. The primary motions are wrist flexion and pronation. Radial deviation is also present. Extension and ulnar deviation are minor secondary motions. All limits are constrained by the ligamentous stability of the joint. The exact feasible ranges for these secondary motions are uncertain and **require future empirical measurement** to define the safe operational limits of the wrist model under the unique loading conditions of arm wrestling.

## Integrated Recommendations and Synthesis

This research report has systematically developed a biomechanical framework tailored for a real-time arm-wrestling simulator, with a primary focus on computational efficiency for control loops exceeding 500 Hz. The analysis has yielded two distinct, complementary deliverables: a recommended minimal muscle model for real-time implementation and an advanced future model for offline validation, alongside a definitive skeletal-joint specification grounded in standardized anatomical references and biomechanical stability principles. The final recommendations are synthesized here to provide a clear and actionable path forward for the project.

The cornerstone of the real-time simulation is the **minimal muscle model**. After a thorough comparison of modeling paradigms, a simplified, excitation-driven Hill-type model was selected as the optimal choice. This model prioritizes computational speed by strategically sacrificing certain physiological details. The key components of this model are: an excitation-driven formulation that bypasses the costly integration of activation dynamics ODEs, thereby directly using a neural input signal `E`; a computationally efficient Gaussian function to model the force-length relationship; a linear approximation of the force-velocity relationship to avoid complex hyperbolic calculations; and, critically, a rigid tendon approximation to eliminate the kinematic complexity of modeling series elastic compliance. While this model will introduce errors in the absolute magnitude of predicted forces, particularly during dynamic contractions, its primary strength lies in its ability to generate plausible, responsive muscle forces at the high frequency required for real-time control. The validation of these force predictions against a more detailed model will be essential for assessing the impact of these simplifications on the overall fidelity of the simulation.

For rigorous verification and refinement, an **advanced future muscle model** is recommended for offline analysis. The most physiologically detailed option is a Huxley-type cross-bridge model. This model simulates the fundamental biochemical process of actin-myosin interaction, offering the potential to capture complex phenomena like short-range stiffness and history-dependent force changes that are beyond the scope of Hill-type models [[5](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1014748), [28](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2896253/)]. Using this model in an offline environment would allow for a direct comparison of muscle force outputs and activation patterns with those generated by the real-time simulator. This would enable the developers to quantify the accuracy of the minimal model and identify specific scenarios where its simplifications lead to significant deviations. As a more accessible alternative for offline validation, an extended Hill-type model incorporating features like separate concentric/eccentric force-velocity curves, viscoelastic damping, and length-dependent activation could be developed. This would offer a substantial improvement in physiological realism over the minimal model without the extreme computational demands of a full Huxley simulation.

Complementing the muscle models is the **definitive skeletal and joint specification**. This specification establishes a consistent and anatomically correct kinematic chain for the upper limb, from the glenohumeral joint through the elbow, radioulnar, and wrist joints. The framework is built upon the standardized Joint Coordinate Systems (JCS) recommended by the International Society of Biomechanics (ISB), ensuring scientific rigor and compatibility with broader biomechanical research [[12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15844264/), [14](https://www.academia.edu/11753608/ISB_recommendation_on_definitions_of_joint_coordinate_systems_of_various_joints_for_the_reporting_of_human_joint_motion_Part_II_shoulder_elbow_wrist_and_hand)]. A central aspect of this specification is the careful differentiation between the full anatomical ROM and the "feasible ROM" relevant to the sport of arm wrestling. Where direct evidence for sport-specific limits was unavailable, the feasible ROM was conservatively constrained by documented general joint stability principles, such as the functional arcs required for activities of daily living and the protective roles of key ligaments like the MCL and LUCL [[16](https://musculoskeletalkey.com/biomechanics-of-the-upper-limb/), [17](https://openorthopaedicsjournal.com/VOLUME/14/PAGE/95/FULLTEXT/)]. This approach avoids inventing arbitrary values and instead grounds the model's operational limits in established biomechanical principles. Any remaining degrees of freedom for which constraints could not be definitively established were flagged as requiring future empirical measurement, acknowledging the boundaries of the current knowledge base and providing a clear direction for subsequent experimental work.

In summary, the proposed framework provides a robust and pragmatic solution. By adopting a highly simplified Hill-type muscle model, the project can meet its stringent real-time performance requirements. Concurrently, the use of a detailed Huxley-type or extended Hill-type model for offline validation ensures that the simulation's outputs can be scrutinized for physiological plausibility. The entire system is anchored by a skeletal specification derived from standardized anatomical references and conservative biomechanical constraints, creating a virtual arm-wrestling system that is both computationally viable and scientifically defensible. This dual-model, empirically-grounded approach represents the most effective strategy for developing a high-fidelity real-time biomechanical simulator.
