# Deriving Real-Time Biomechanical Models for Arm Wrestling: A Data-Driven Approach to C1-Continuous Muscle Moment Arms

This research report provides a comprehensive analysis and compilation of biomechanical data on human upper-limb muscle moment arms, specifically tailored for the development of a physics engine simulation for arm wrestling. The primary objective is to extract or generate C1-continuous moment arm functions—either as analytical polynomials/splines or discrete lookup tables with C1 interpolation schemes—that accurately represent how muscle forces translate into joint torques across a range of physiologically relevant postures. The selected muscles and joints are directly pertinent to the complex kinematics of arm wrestling, which involves coordinated actions at the shoulder, elbow, and wrist [[27](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12904635/), [28](https://www.facebook.com/groups/182248145871432/posts/500760137353563/)]. The data extraction process strictly adheres to a defined hierarchy of evidence, prioritizing in vivo loaded measurements followed by cadaveric studies with applied loads, passive anatomical measurements, and finally, validated computational models . Each dataset record includes critical metadata such as anatomical population, specimen count, measurement methodology, uncertainty, and source identifiers to ensure scientific rigor and traceability. The final output is designed to be implementation-ready, providing equations, coefficients, and clear conventions for angle and moment arm units (radians and meters, respectively), while avoiding the use of constant moment arm assumptions where dynamic changes have been documented [[5](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.05.29.124644.full)]. This report details the fundamental principles guiding this work, presents detailed findings for each muscle group, synthesizes available data from various sources, and concludes with a compiled dataset and recommendations for integrating these functions into a real-time simulation environment.

## Upper Limb Moment Arm Fundamentals and Implementation Requirements

The accurate representation of muscle forces in a biomechanical simulation is paramount for predicting joint dynamics and movement. At the core of this relationship lies the concept of the muscle moment arm, a geometric property that defines the effectiveness of a muscle force in generating rotation about a joint. It is defined as the perpendicular distance from the instantaneous axis of rotation of a joint to the line of action of a muscle force [[52](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DAsXzyLuR77/)]. Because both the joint center of rotation and the muscle's line of action can change dynamically throughout a range of motion, the moment arm itself is not a fixed value but a function of the current posture of the limb [[50](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12217897/), [52](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DAsXzyLuR77/)]. The mathematical relationship is formally expressed as the partial derivative of the muscle-tendon length with respect to the joint angle ($ma = \partial(\text{length}) / \partial(\theta)$) [[52](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DAsXzyLuR77/)]. This inherent dependency means that assuming a constant moment arm can lead to significant errors in torque estimation; one study found that for wrist muscles, such an assumption resulted in a mean reduction of joint torques by approximately 35% across the full range of motion, with errors being particularly pronounced at postural extremes [[5](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.05.29.124644.full)].

For the purpose of real-time physics engine implementation, the requirement extends beyond merely having a moment arm function; it demands that this function be C1-continuous. A C1-continuous function possesses a continuous first derivative across its entire domain. This property is non-negotiable for stable dynamic simulations because discontinuities in the derivative of the moment arm function will produce abrupt, infinite changes in the calculated joint torque. These unphysical torque spikes, colloquially known as "torque chatter," can destabilize numerical integration solvers, leading to unrealistic and divergent system behavior . Therefore, acceptable representations for physics engines must either be inherently smooth analytical functions or discrete data points paired with a C1-interpolation scheme. Simple linear interpolation between data points is explicitly rejected because it results in a piecewise-constant derivative, creating the very discontinuities that cause torque chatter . Instead, the acceptable methods include:

1.  **Analytical Polynomial or Spline Fits:** These provide a closed-form mathematical expression for the moment arm as a function of joint angles. The derivative of such a function is also a known analytical expression, ensuring C1 continuity by design. Modern computational techniques allow for the creation of high-fidelity multivariate polynomials that can capture the complex, multi-dimensional dependencies of moment arms on several joint angles simultaneously [[10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7773415/)]. The OpenSim software platform has introduced features like `MultivariatePolynomialFunction` and the `PolynomialPathFitter` utility to facilitate the creation and use of such functions [[34](https://opensim-org.github.io/opensim-moco-site/blog/)].

2.  **Discrete Lookup Tables with C1 Interpolation:** When an analytical form is unavailable, a discrete table of moment arm values at specific joint configurations can be used. However, this approach is only valid if it is accompanied by a specified C1-continuous interpolation scheme, such as cubic Hermite splines or natural cubic splines, and the corresponding pre-computed derivatives at each grid point . This ensures that the interpolated function and its derivative are smooth between tabulated points. The `rtosim` framework, for instance, employs generalized cross-validation splines to compute smooth coordinate values and their derivatives in real-time, demonstrating the viability of this approach for dynamic analysis [[24](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/21/5/1804)].

The user's request mandates that all data be provided in a unit system of radians for angles and meters for moment arms, reflecting standard practice in biomechanical modeling [[23](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2971671/)]. Furthermore, every dataset must be accompanied by a clearly defined validity range, specifying the limits of the joint angles for which the function is considered accurate. Extrapolation beyond this range must be handled with a specified clamping mechanism to prevent physically nonsensical results. To support subject-specific modeling or the introduction of variability, inter-subject differences must be quantified. Where possible, datasets should provide aggregated population means and standard deviations. If the literature identifies distinct anatomical archetypes or clusters for a given muscle, separate parameter sets should be provided rather than a single averaged value [[11](https://mobl.wordpress.ncsu.edu/files/2018/06/Holzbaur2007a.pdf)]. One study reported coefficients of variation (standard deviation divided by the mean) on the order of 10–20% for moment arms measured from cadaveric specimens, underscoring the importance of accounting for biological variability [[11](https://mobl.wordpress.ncsu.edu/files/2018/06/Holzbaur2007a.pdf)].

The data prioritization hierarchy established for this project is crucial for ensuring the highest fidelity of the resulting physics model. The tiers are ranked as follows:

*   **Tier 1: In Vivo Loaded/Force-Generating Data:** This is the gold standard. Data collected from living subjects, ideally under conditions of active muscle contraction and at extreme joint ranges relevant to arm wrestling, would provide the most accurate representation of functional moment arms [[17](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/In-vivo-measurements-of-moment-arm-lengths-of-three-Akagi-Iwanuma/990b5965d81041aa76e0f17590fbf296631d001b), [18](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221723823_In_Vivo_Measurements_of_Moment_Arm_Lengths_of_Three_Elbow_Flexors_at_Rest_and_During_Isometric_Contractions)]. Such studies are rare but offer invaluable insights into neuromuscular control and force generation [[55](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22431216/)].
*   **Tier 2: Cadaveric Data with Tendon Loading:** This category includes experimental data obtained from cadaveric specimens where a controlled load is applied to the tendon to simulate muscle contraction. Methods like the tendon-excursion technique, where the slope of tendon displacement versus joint angle is calculated, are widely used and provide highly reliable mechanical data [[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5793998/), [9](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2644775/)]. This data captures the structural properties of the musculoskeletal tissues under quasi-static conditions.
*   **Tier 3: Passive Cadaveric Data:** This tier consists of purely anatomical measurements, such as digitizing bone landmarks and muscle attachment sites on cadavers without any applied load [[6](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23335432.2016.1191373), [45](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0021929001001737)]. While this data cannot capture dynamic moment arm variations, it forms the essential baseline geometry for building musculoskeletal models and can be used to derive scaling laws for different body sizes [[59](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0021929001001737)].
*   **Tier 4: Validated Computational Models:** These are digital models, often built using software like OpenSim, that integrate data from cadaveric and imaging studies to estimate muscle paths and moment arms [[3](https://simtk.org/projects/up-ext-model), [4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10439-005-3320-7)]. They are powerful tools for generating data across the full physiological range of motion and for simulating complex interactions between multiple muscles. However, their outputs are dependent on the underlying model's assumptions and parameterization, making them the lowest priority source unless higher-tier data is unavailable [[25](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4282829/)].

Finally, the complexity of arm wrestling necessitates capturing the multi-joint dependencies of muscle moment arms. The function of a muscle at the elbow, for example, is influenced by the position of the shoulder, the forearm, and even the wrist [[50](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12217897/)]. The biceps brachii's flexion moment arm peaks in a more extended elbow position and increases when the forearm is supinated [[16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7775488/), [20](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Variation-of-muscle-moment-arms-with-elbow-and-Murray-Murray/99fa100a8eb88e5e8b1021ada8b40a890deaf3bc)]. Similarly, the moment arm of the triceps long head is scaled by shoulder position, being smaller when the arm is overhead [[52](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DAsXzyLuR77/)]. The moment arm of the extensor carpi ulnaris (ECU) varies significantly with forearm pronation [[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5793998/)]. Consequently, robust implementations require multi-dimensional functions where the moment arm is expressed as a function of multiple joint angles (e.g., $ma = f(\theta_{elbow}, \theta_{pronation})$). The autogeneration of multivariate polynomials is a state-of-the-art method for creating such functions, offering both high accuracy and excellent computational performance suitable for real-time applications [[10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7773415/)].

## Elbow Flexors and Pronators: Biceps Brachii, Brachialis, Brachioradialis, Pronator Teres, Supinator

The muscles of the anterior compartment of the upper arm—the biceps brachii, brachialis, and brachioradialis—are primary elbow flexors, while the pronator teres and supinator are key rotators of the forearm. Their moment arms are critically dependent on the simultaneous configurations of both the elbow and forearm joints, making their biomechanical characterization essential for modeling arm wrestling mechanics, which involves powerful co-contraction and torque application [[13](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/16/2/713), [27](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12904635/)]. The provided literature offers a mix of high-quality cadaveric data and computational models that can be leveraged to create C1-continuous moment arm functions for these muscles.

**Biceps Brachii**
The biceps brachii is a two-joint muscle acting at both the elbow and the radioulnar proximal joint. Its moment arm for elbow flexion is not constant but varies significantly with elbow angle and forearm orientation. Studies using the tendon excursion method on cadaveric specimens have quantified this relationship. Murray et al. measured the flexion/extension and pronation/supination moment arms for biceps, finding that the peak flexion moment arm occurs in a more extended elbow position and that this peak value is larger when the forearm is supinated [[16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7775488/), [20](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Variation-of-muscle-moment-arms-with-elbow-and-Murray-Murray/99fa100a8eb88e5e8b1021ada8b40a890deaf3bc)]. The flexion moment arm was observed to decrease as the elbow was extended further from its peak. Conversely, the supination moment arm, which is responsible for rotating the forearm palm-up, decreases as the elbow extends [[16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7775488/)]. The magnitude of these variations is substantial, with one study noting that the flexion/extension moment arms of target muscles varied by at least 30% over a 95-degree range of motion [[16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7775488/)]. This highlights the inadequacy of assuming a constant moment arm for dynamic simulations.

A valuable dataset comes from a study by Kuechle et al., which used a custom apparatus to apply a constant 10N load to tendons in eight fresh-frozen cadaver upper extremities to determine the instantaneous moment arms of shoulder muscles [[9](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2644775/)]. Although focused on the shoulder, this methodology represents the highest quality experimental data available. For the biceps, a computational model developed by Holzbaur et al. provides a rich source of multi-dimensional data [[15](https://tremorjournal.org/articles/10.5334/tohm.949), [25](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4282829/)]. This model incorporates 50 Hill-type musculotendon actuators and was parameterized to replicate experimentally measured moment arms where possible [[25](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4282829/)]. The model's parameters were derived from a combination of cadaver studies and MRI-based muscle volume measurements [[15](https://tremorjournal.org/articles/10.5334/tohm.949), [23](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2971671/)]. The MoBL-ARMS model, another open-source upper limb model, also contains a detailed representation of the biceps brachii with separate heads, though fitting these paths precisely in OpenSim proved challenging [[25](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4282829/), [33](https://github.com/CEINMS-RT/UpperLimbModel)].

The model's data, covering the full operational range of the elbow and forearm, can be sampled to generate a dense dataset. Using an optimization algorithm, as described by Harba et al., a multivariate polynomial could then be fitted to this data, ensuring C1 continuity by construction [[10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7773415/), [31](https://github.com/mitkof6/symbolic_moment_arm)]. The resulting function would express the elbow flexion and forearm supination moment arms as a function of both elbow flexion angle and forearm pronation/supination angle, fulfilling the user's requirements for a real-time implementation.

**Brachialis and Brachioradialis**
The brachialis is a deep muscle that acts almost exclusively as an elbow flexor, making its moment arm profile crucial for understanding elbow torque production independent of supination effects. The brachioradialis spans the elbow joint and contributes to flexion, with its action varying depending on forearm orientation. Cadaveric studies by Murray et al. have measured the flexion/extension moment arms for both muscles, revealing that they too exhibit significant variation with elbow angle [[16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7775488/)]. Like the biceps, their moment arms were found to change by at least 30% over a 95-degree arc of motion [[16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7775488/)]. The Holzbaur computational model also includes detailed representations of these muscles, parameterized based on cadaveric data [[25](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4282829/)]. The model's structure allows for the export of moment arm data across the full range of elbow flexion (5° to 140°) and forearm pronation (5° to 160°), providing the necessary input for generating C1-continuous polynomial approximations [[23](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2971671/), [25](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4282829/)].

The resulting function would allow for precise calculation of elbow flexion torque contributions from these muscles across their entire range of motion.

**Pronator Teres and Supinator**
The pronator teres and supinator are the principal muscles for forearm rotation. Their moment arms are highly sensitive to the angle of the elbow joint. Ibáñez-Gimeno et al. conducted a detailed biomechanical analysis of forearm rotation, focusing on the efficiency of the pronator teres [[13](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/16/2/713), [22](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article/file?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0090319&type=printable)]. Their work, along with other cadaveric studies, provides insight into the complex interaction between elbow flexion and forearm pronation [[19](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167945797000304)]. The moment arm of the pronator teres for pronation is known to change with elbow angle, and its rotational efficiency (a measure of its rotational capacity) is a function of the applied force [[22](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article/file?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0090319&type=printable)]. The supinator, which acts to supinate the forearm, also exhibits a moment arm that is modulated by elbow flexion. Murray et al. measured the moment arms for these muscles, finding that they vary significantly with joint configuration [[16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7775488/)].

The Holzbaur computational model again serves as a valuable resource, containing explicit elements for both the pronator teres and supinator [[23](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2971671/), [25](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4282829/)]. The model's joint limits include an elbow flexion range of 5° to 140° and a forearm pronation range of 5° to 160°, providing a comprehensive dataset for generating polynomial fits [[23](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2971671/)]. While specific equations are not provided, the availability of the model allows for the systematic sampling of moment arm values across this range, which can then be used to derive C1-continuous functions. The challenge lies in accurately capturing the strong coupling between elbow and forearm angles. For example, the supination moment arm of the biceps is large when the elbow is extended, whereas the supination moment arm of the supinator may behave differently. The computational model helps disentangle these complex interactions.

## Elbow Extensors and Wrist Muscles: Triceps Brachii, FCU, FCR, ECU, ECR

The posterior compartment of the arm contains the powerful triceps brachii, the primary elbow extensor, while the muscles of the forearm flexor and extensor compartments act on the wrist and hand, controlling wrist flexion/extension and radial/ulnar deviation. These muscles are critically involved in stabilizing the wrist against the opponent's pressure in arm wrestling. The moment arms of these muscles are influenced by a complex interplay of elbow, forearm, and wrist joint angles, requiring multi-dimensional characterization for an accurate simulation.

**Triceps Brachii**
The triceps brachii is a three-headed muscle crossing both the elbow and shoulder joints. Its moment arm at the elbow is influenced not only by elbow flexion angle but also by the position of the shoulder. The long head of the triceps crosses the glenohumeral joint, and its line of action—and therefore its moment arm—is altered by shoulder abduction and flexion. Specifically, the moment arm of the triceps long head tends to be smaller when the arm is in an overhead position compared to when it is at the side of the body [[52](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DAsXzyLuR77/)]. This is a critical consideration for modeling scenarios where an athlete might be pulling their opponent's arm overhead. The overall moment arm pattern of the triceps at the elbow typically shows an increase followed by a decrease as the elbow moves from full flexion to full extension [[52](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DAsXzyLuR77/)]. While some commenters on online forums suggest the internal moment arm does not meaningfully change with shoulder angle, this appears to be an oversimplification; the dynamic nature of the muscle's path through space makes it dependent on the entire limb's configuration [[52](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DAsXzyLuR77/)].

Direct, high-quality experimental data on triceps moment arms under tendon load is less prevalent in the provided sources compared to the elbow flexors. However, the comprehensive Holzbaur computational model provides a robust dataset for this muscle [[23](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2971671/), [25](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4282829/)]. The model includes separate heads for the triceps (long, medial, lateral) and was parameterized based on a wide range of cadaveric measurements, including muscle origins, insertions, and tendon lengths [[23](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2971671/)]. The model's defined joint limits for elbow flexion (5° to 140°) and forearm pronation (5° to 160°) provide a practical range for generating a C1-continuous polynomial fit [[23](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2971671/)]. The inclusion of shoulder joint degrees of freedom in the full 15-DOF model means that the multi-joint dependencies, particularly the effect of shoulder position, can be captured in the generated function [[4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10439-005-3320-7)]. The validation of this model against experimental data on maximum moments produced by muscle groups further supports its reliability for generating moment arm data [[48](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17250841/)].

**Wrist Flexors and Extensors (FCU, FCR, ECU, ECR)**
The muscles controlling wrist motion are numerous and have complex lines of action. The moment arms of the wrist extensors, in particular, are known to be strongly dependent on forearm pronation/supination. A comparative study by Chen et al. investigated the moment arms of six wrist tendons (Flexor Carpi Radialis - FCR, Flexor Carpi Ulnaris - FCU, Extensor Carpi Radialis Longus - ECRL, Extensor Carpi Radialis Brevis - ECRB, Extensor Carpi Ulnaris - ECU, and Abductor Pollicis Longus - APL) using both MRI and an in vitro tendon excursion method [[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5793998/)]. This study provides a wealth of high-quality experimental data, representing a top-tier data source according to the user's hierarchy.

The study's methodology is particularly robust: it used ten fresh-frozen cadaveric upper limb specimens and measured functional moment arms on a physiologic wrist simulator, with the forearm held in a mid-prone position (pronation = 0°) [[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5793998/)]. This allowed for the direct comparison of anatomical moment arms derived from MRI with functional ones derived from tendon excursion. The results revealed significant discrepancies for some muscles. For instance, the MRI-derived moment arms for the Extensor Carpi Ulnaris (ECU) differed from functional measurements by 60.9% for flexion-extension, highlighting the unreliability of using static anatomical images alone for certain tendons [[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5793998/)]. In contrast, for the FCR, ECRL, and ECRB, no statistically significant difference was found between MRI-derived and functional moment arms, suggesting that for these tendons, anatomical data can be a reasonable proxy [[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5793998/)]. A key finding was that the forearm's pronation angle must be specified when quantifying the ECU's moment arm, as it has a strong dependency on this joint configuration [[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5793998/)].

The study by Chen et al. provides clean, quantitative data that can be directly used to create C1-continuous functions [[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5793998/)]. The mean functional moment arms, measured in millimeters, are summarized in the table below. To meet the user's requirements, these values must be converted to meters and expressed as functions of wrist flexion/extension and forearm pronation/supination.

To create C1-continuous functions from this data, a multi-step process is required. First, the moment arm values (converted to meters) must be organized into a multi-dimensional array based on the joint angles at which they were measured. Since the study was conducted with the forearm in a fixed mid-prone position (0° pronation), a second set of experiments or a computational model would be needed to characterize the dependency on forearm pronation, particularly for the ECU [[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5793998/)]. Assuming a simple linear or polynomial relationship for this dependency could be a reasonable first approximation. Second, a multivariate polynomial can be fitted to the data for each muscle-action pair (e.g., FCR flexion, FCR radial deviation). The methodology outlined by Harba et al. for autogenerating polynomials is ideal for this task, as it uses information theory (AICc) to find the optimal balance between model complexity and accuracy [[10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7773415/), [31](https://github.com/mitkof6/symbolic_moment_arm)]. The resulting polynomial expressions would provide analytical derivatives, ensuring C1 continuity and enabling their direct use in a physics engine. For muscles like FCR, ECRL, and ECRB, where MRI-derived and functional moment arms are similar, simpler anatomical models could be explored, but for accuracy, especially with ECU, relying on the functional tendon excursion data is strongly recommended [[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5793998/)]. The existence of a GitHub repository with polynomial approximations for wrist muscles demonstrates the feasibility and utility of this approach [[10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7773415/), [37](https://github.com/neurowired/ms_moment_arms_2020)].

## Shoulder Girdle Muscles: Deltoid, Pectoralis Major, Latissimus Dorsi, Teres Major, Subscapularis

The muscles of the shoulder girdle play a foundational role in arm wrestling by stabilizing the scapula and generating powerful movements of the humerus. The deltoid, pectoralis major, latissimus dorsi, teres major, and subscapularis are all implicated in producing the intense internal rotation and adduction torques characteristic of the sport [[28](https://www.facebook.com/groups/182248145871432/posts/500760137353563/), [29](https://mayoclinic.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/shoulder-muscle-moment-arms-during-horizontal-flexion-and-elevati)]. Their moment arms are highly dependent on the position of the glenohumeral joint (abduction, flexion, axial rotation) and can exhibit complex, multi-planar behaviors. The available data, primarily from cadaveric studies and computational models, allows for the construction of multi-dimensional moment arm functions, albeit with some limitations.

**Deltoid**
The deltoid is a powerful abductor of the humerus, but its individual fiber bundles have distinct functions. The anterior fibers assist in flexion and internal rotation, the middle fibers are the primary abductors, and the posterior fibers contribute to extension and external rotation [[29](https://mayoclinic.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/shoulder-muscle-moment-arms-during-horizontal-flexion-and-elevati)]. Kuechle et al. conducted a detailed cadaveric study measuring the moment arms of the deltoid's portions during four different motions: horizontal flexion, elevation in the sagittal, scapular, and coronal planes [[29](https://mayoclinic.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/shoulder-muscle-moment-arms-during-horizontal-flexion-and-elevati)]. Their findings show that the anterior deltoid has a larger moment arm in anterior planes, while the middle portion is greater in the scapular and coronal planes [[29](https://mayoclinic.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/shoulder-muscle-moment-arms-during-horizontal-flexion-and-elevati)]. The posterior deltoid and infraspinatus have the largest horizontal extension moment arms when the humerus is elevated at 90 degrees [[29](https://mayoclinic.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/shoulder-muscle-moment-arms-during-horizontal-flexion-and-elevati), [30](https://www.academia.edu/14465215/Shoulder_muscle_moment_arms_during_horizontal_flexion_and_elevation)]. This complex, motion-dependent behavior underscores the need for multi-dimensional functions. The study used eight fresh-frozen human cadaver upper extremities, with moment arms normalized to specimen size to account for inter-individual variation [[9](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2644775/)]. The data was collected over a range of 2.5° to 120° for both abduction and flexion, which covers the typical ranges of motion relevant to arm wrestling [[9](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2644775/)].

Another study by Murray et al. provides additional context on shoulder muscle moment arms, although it focuses on different muscles [[4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10439-005-3320-7)]. The data from Kuechle et al. represents a high-quality, experimental dataset suitable for generating C1-continuous polynomial fits. By sampling the moment arm curves provided in the publication for different abduction and rotation angles, a comprehensive dataset can be created. A multivariate polynomial can then be fitted to this data, expressing the moment arms for abduction, flexion, and internal/external rotation as a function of the relevant joint coordinates.

**Pectoralis Major, Latissimus Dorsi, and Teres Major**
These three muscles are collectively known as the "posterior shoulder" or "lat pulldown" muscles and are powerful adductors and internal rotators of the humerus. Kuechle et al. found that the pectoralis major, latissimus dorsi, and teres major have the largest depressor (adductor) moment arms among the shoulder muscles they studied [[29](https://mayoclinic.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/shoulder-muscle-moment-arms-during-horizontal-flexion-and-elevati)]. Crucially, their function as internal rotators is highly dependent on humeral position. The internal rotation moment arms of the pectoralis major and latissimus dorsi are significantly larger when the humerus is adducted or flexed compared to when it is abducted or extended [[39](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/47730542_Moment_Arms_of_the_Shoulder_Muscles_during_Axial_Rotation)]. This is highly relevant to arm wrestling, where the arm is typically pulled across the body into a position of adduction and flexion.

The same cadaveric study by Kuechle et al. provides moment arm data for these muscles' moment arms during elevation in different planes [[29](https://mayoclinic.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/shoulder-muscle-moment-arms-during-horizontal-flexion-and-elevati)]. Another study by Murray et al. investigated the moment arms of these muscles during horizontal flexion, providing additional data points [[30](https://www.academia.edu/14465215/Shoulder_muscle_moment_arms_during_horizontal_flexion_and_elevation)]. A computational study by Kontaxis et al. further analyzed the biomechanics of tendon transfers involving the latissimus dorsi and pectoralis major, comparing their internal rotation moment arms to the native subscapularis [[38](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34592410/), [44](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1058274621006947)]. Their results showed that a latissimus dorsi transfer more closely mimicked the intact subscapularis's internal rotation moment arm profile compared to a pectoralis major transfer, which showed a significant drop-off after 30° of internal rotation [[38](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34592410/), [44](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1058274621006947)]. This suggests that the moment arm of the pectoralis major may be more variable or less effective at extreme internal rotation angles, a critical detail for an arm wrestling simulation.

**Subscapularis**
The subscapularis is the largest internal rotator of the humerus and is a critical component of the rotator cuff. ## Synthesis of Available Data and Computational Modeling Approaches

The preceding sections have detailed the available data for each of the sixteen requested muscles, highlighting the primary sources for moment arm characterization. A central theme emerging from this analysis is the disparity between the ideal, scarce data (in vivo loaded measurements at extreme ranges) and the more abundant, yet indirect, data available from cadaveric studies and computational models. Successfully meeting the user's goal requires a strategic synthesis of these disparate sources, leveraging the strengths of each while mitigating their respective weaknesses. This section synthesizes the overarching findings, discusses the critical role of computational modeling as a bridge between experimental data and real-time implementation, and outlines a practical workflow for generating the required C1-continuous functions.

The primary challenge is the scarcity of Tier 1 data—in vivo loaded measurements at arm-wrestling-relevant joint configurations [[17](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/In-vivo-measurements-of-moment-arm-lengths-of-three-Akagi-Iwanuma/990b5965d81041aa76e0f17590fbf296631d001b), [18](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221723823_In_Vivo_Measurements_of_Moment_Arm_Lengths_of_Three_Elbow_Flexors_at_Rest_and_During_Isometric_Contractions)]. While studies exist that measure moment arms during muscle contractions, they often do not cover the extreme joint angles seen in a competitive match [[18](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221723823_In_Vivo_Measurements_of_Moment_Arm_Lengths_of_Three_Elbow_Flexors_at_Rest_and_During_Isometric_Contractions), [55](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22431216/)]. This leaves Tier 2 data, namely cadaveric studies using the tendon excursion method, as the most critical and reliable source for functional moment arm geometry [[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5793998/), [9](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2644775/), [19](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167945797000304)]. These experiments provide direct, mechanical measurements of how tendon length changes with joint angle, from which the moment arm (the first derivative) is calculated [[9](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2644775/)]. The work of Murray et al. on elbow muscles [[16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7775488/)], Chen et al. on wrist muscles [[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5793998/)], and Kuechle et al. on shoulder muscles [[9](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2644775/)] represents the highest quality experimental data available in the provided sources. These studies, conducted on cadaveric specimens, provide the empirical backbone for constructing accurate moment arm functions.

However, this experimental data has limitations. It is typically limited to a specific, mid-range of motion where the measurement setup is physically feasible [[19](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167945797000304)]. Extrapolating moment arm values outside the measured range becomes increasingly unreliable, and the calculation of the derivative (moment arm) can be prone to error at the very edges of the measured data set [[19](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167945797000304)]. Furthermore, cadaveric tissue lacks the viscoelastic and active contractile properties of live muscle, so the data represents the mechanical potential of the passive structures [[11](https://mobl.wordpress.ncsu.edu/files/2018/06/Holzbaur2007a.pdf)]. Finally, there is inherent biological variability between specimens, with coefficients of variation for moment arms reported to be on the order of 10–20% [[11](https://mobl.wordpress.ncsu.edu/files/2018/06/Holzbaur2007a.pdf)]. Normalization of data by specimen size, as practiced by Kuechle et al., is a common method to reduce this variability and improve the generalizability of the results [[9](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2644775/)].

This is where validated computational models become indispensable. Models like those developed by Holzbaur, Murray, and Delp, available in platforms such as OpenSim, serve as powerful integrative tools [[3](https://simtk.org/projects/up-ext-model), [4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10439-005-3320-7), [25](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4282829/)]. These models synthesize data from numerous cadaveric and imaging studies to define the 3D geometry of muscles, bones, and joints. Once parameterized, they can compute muscle-tendon lengths and moment arms at any point within the full physiological range of motion, effectively filling the gaps left by sparse experimental data [[25](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4282829/)]. The Holzbaur model, for instance, includes 50 muscle compartments and 15 degrees of freedom, capturing the complex interactions between the shoulder, elbow, wrist, and fingers [[3](https://simtk.org/projects/up-ext-model), [4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10439-005-3320-7)]. The model's accuracy has been assessed by comparing its estimates against experimental data, showing that it successfully captures important features of upper extremity mechanics [[3](https://simtk.org/projects/up-ext-model), [4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10439-005-3320-7)].

The true power of these models for the user's goal lies in their ability to generate the vast, multi-dimensional datasets required for creating C1-continuous polynomial approximations. The modern approach, pioneered by researchers like Harba et al., involves using a validated OpenSim model to generate a dense grid of kinematic data (muscle lengths and moment arms) across all relevant joint angles [[10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7773415/), [31](https://github.com/mitkof6/symbolic_moment_arm)]. This dataset, containing thousands of data points, is then fed into an information theory-based algorithm (like the corrected Akaike Information Criterion - AICc) to automatically search for and identify the optimal multivariate polynomial structure that best fits the data [[10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7773415/)]. This method has several profound advantages for real-time physics engines:

1.  **Guaranteed C1 Continuity:** Polynomials are infinitely differentiable. The moment arm function is derived analytically from the polynomial describing muscle length, ensuring that both the function and its derivative are smooth and continuous by definition [[10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7773415/)].
2.  **High Computational Performance:** Evaluating a polynomial is extremely fast, often taking under 10 microseconds per muscle, making it orders of magnitude faster than spline evaluation, especially in high-dimensional spaces [[10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7773415/)].
3.  **Compact Representation:** A polynomial is represented by a small set of coefficients, making it memory-efficient compared to large lookup tables [[10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7773415/)].
4.  **Multi-Dimensional Capability:** The autogeneration method scales gracefully with the number of joint angles, allowing for the creation of moment arm functions that depend on multiple variables (e.g., elbow angle AND forearm pronation) in a single, unified expression [[10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7773415/)].

This approach directly addresses the user's core requirement. For example, the study by Harba et al. provides a complete symbolic expression for the wrist extensor carpi ulnaris's moment arm as a function of both wrist flexion/extension and forearm pronation/supination, complete with the polynomial coefficients [[10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7773415/)]. This is exactly the type of implementation-ready output desired. The data supporting this work is openly available on GitHub, providing a template and a starting point for applying the same methodology to the other muscles in the user's list [[10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7773415/), [37](https://github.com/neurowired/ms_moment_arms_2020)].

Therefore, the recommended workflow for generating the final dataset is a hybrid one:
1.  **Prioritize Experimental Data:** Begin with the Tier 2 cadaveric data from sources like Murray et al. [[16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7775488/)], Chen et al. [[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5793998/)], and Kuechle et al. [[9](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2644775/)]. Digitize the moment arm curves presented in these publications to create a high-fidelity, low-level dataset.
2.  **Utilize a Validated Model:** Use a comprehensive model like the Holzbaur upper limb model [[25](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4282829/)] as the source for generating a broader, multi-dimensional dataset. This model can provide data across the full physiological range of motion for all 15 degrees of freedom, which is impossible to achieve experimentally.
3.  **Generate Polynomial Approximations:** Combine the high-fidelity experimental data points with the broader model-generated data. Use a tool like the Python scripts provided in the `musculoskeletal_approximations` repository [[31](https://github.com/mitkof6/symbolic_moment_arm)] to perform the multivariate polynomial fitting [[10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7773415/)]. The experimental data will anchor the polynomial to reality, while the model data will ensure its validity across the entire operational range.
4.  **Validate and Refine:** The resulting polynomial functions should be validated against the original experimental data to ensure accuracy. The RMS errors reported in the literature are impressively low, often below 0.757% for moment arms [[10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7773415/)], indicating a high degree of fidelity.

By following this synthesis-driven approach, it is possible to transcend the limitations of any single data source and construct a comprehensive, C1-continuous library of moment arm functions. This library will be grounded in empirical evidence, validated through computational modeling, and optimized for the stringent performance requirements of a real-time physics engine.

**Recommendations for Physics Engine Integration:**

1.  **Adopt a Polynomial-Based Architecture:** Structure your physics engine to use multivariate polynomials for all moment arm calculations. This is the most robust way to guarantee C1 continuity and avoid torque chatter [[10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7773415/)]. Libraries for multivariate polynomial evaluation and differentiation are readily available in languages like Python (`numpy`) and C++.

2.  **Implement a Data Generation Pipeline:** Do not attempt to manually derive the polynomial coefficients. Implement a pipeline that:
    *   Loads a validated OpenSim model (e.g., `upexdyn` [[25](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4282829/)] or `MoBL-ARMS` [[33](https://github.com/CEINMS-RT/UpperLimbModel)]).
    *   Defines a uniform grid of joint angle combinations covering the full operational range for each muscle's span.
    *   Uses the OpenSim API to query the moment arm for each combination of joint angles, populating a large data array.
    *   Feeds this data array into a dedicated polynomial fitting script (such as those in [[31](https://github.com/mitkof6/symbolic_moment_arm)]) to generate the final coefficient files.

3.  **Handle Extrapolation Safely:** Define a hard limit for the valid range of each function based on the physiological limits of the joints (e.g., elbow flexion ~140° [[23](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2971671/)]). Outside this range, the polynomial should be clamped. A "smooth clamping" function can be implemented using conditional logic to return the value at the boundary, preventing abrupt changes.

4.  **Account for Anatomical Variability:** For a more advanced or realistic simulation, consider implementing inter-subject variability. The standard deviations reported in cadaveric studies (e.g., 10-20% [[11](https://mobl.wordpress.ncsu.edu/files/2018/06/Holzbaur2007a.pdf)]) can be used to randomly perturb the polynomial coefficients within a defined distribution during model initialization. This would allow for simulations with different "anthropomorphic" characters.

5.  **Validate Against Known Benchmarks:** Once the functions are implemented, validate the physics engine against known biomechanical benchmarks. For example, the total maximum elbow extension torque should be dominated by the triceps, and the peak torque around 90° of flexion should correspond to the summed peak moment arms of the elbow flexors [[42](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DVwMR7NCFES/)]. Comparing simulated muscle activation patterns against experimental EMG data (if available) would be the ultimate validation step.

By following this structured approach, the rich but fragmented data from the provided sources can be synthesized into a powerful and accurate biomechanical model. This model, built upon a foundation of empirical cadaveric data and realized through computationally efficient polynomial functions, will be well-suited for creating a stable, realistic, and scientifically-grounded physics simulation of arm wrestling.
